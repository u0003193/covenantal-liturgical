    Chapter 17 — “Do This in Anamnesis of Me” (Luke 22:19–20; 1 Cor 11:23–26)

    Thesis (≤2 sentences):
    Jesus interprets His death as the new covenant meal: “This cup… is the new covenant in my blood,” and commands the Church’s anamnesis—remembrance as participatory re‑presentation—so the once‑for‑all sacrifice becomes lived communion rather than distant past (Luke 22:19–20; 1 Cor 11:23–26). By epiclesis the Spirit is God‑active, actualizing participation in Christ’s offering and forming a priestly people whose Eucharistic life becomes covenantal love and mercy “for the life of the world” (1 Cor 10:16–17; Heb 10:19–22; Heb 13:16).

    Biblical Ground (passages):
    Exod 12:1–14, 24–27; Exod 24:3–11; Exod 25:16–22; Lev 7:11–15; Deut 16:1–8; Isa 53:4–12; Jer 31:31–34; Luke 22:7–20; Luke 24:30–35; John 6:35–58; Acts 2:42; 1 Cor 10:16–17; 1 Cor 11:17–34; Heb 8:6–13; Heb 9:11–15; Heb 10:10–25; Rev 5:6–10; Rev 19:6–9.

    Argument Map (Wilken‑calibrated):
    [Setup/Context → Question‑at‑Issue → Prior Answers (insufficient) → Scripture/Event Anchor → Patristic Turn → Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis) → Payoff/Implications]

    One‑liners for each move (keep it tight)

    • Setup/Context: The Covenant–Temple plot becomes personal in Christ the living Temple; now Christ gives the Church a covenantal-liturgy that makes His once‑for‑all offering present as communion: “Do this” (John 1:14; Luke 22:19).
    • Question‑at‑Issue: How can a singular historical sacrifice become ongoing participation without implying repetition—how can worship be real communion rather than mere mental recall or parallel cult? (Heb 10:10–14; 1 Cor 10:16).
    • Prior Answers (why insufficient): (1) Eucharist as bare symbol/memory; (2) Eucharist as re‑sacrifice; (3) Eucharist as private spirituality—each fails to match the covenant logic of Exodus 24 and Paul’s “participation,” and each ignores epiclesis: the Spirit as God‑active (Exod 24:8–11; 1 Cor 10:16–17; Heb 9:14).
    • Scripture/Event Anchor: Jesus’ words of institution (Luke 22:19–20) and Paul’s handed‑on tradition (1 Cor 11:23–26) define anamnesis as covenant participation; the Church “proclaims the Lord’s death until he comes.”
    • Patristic Turn: Didache and Justin show early Eucharistic anamnesis; Irenaeus grounds realism in creation taken into Christ; Cyril of Jerusalem and Basil articulate Spirit‑invocation (epiclesis); Chrysostom and Augustine insist Eucharist yields ethics—unity, mercy, and self‑offering (1 Cor 11:17–22; Heb 13:16).
    • Doctrinal Resolution: Anamnesis is liturgical participation in the once‑for‑all sacrifice: the Spirit actualizes communion with the enthroned Christ; the Church becomes one Body‑Temple, renewed in covenant and trained in covenantal love (1 Cor 10:16–17; Heb 10:19–25).
    • Payoff/Implications (worship/ethics/formation): The Table is covenant renewal: it forms unity, demands reconciliation, and sends mercy into the world—“liturgy after the liturgy” as Eucharistic ethics (Matt 5:23–24; 1 Cor 11:28–32).

    Patristic Witness (top 6 citations):
    1. Didache 9–10; 14 (Eucharistic thanksgiving, unity, Lord’s Day practice; early anamnesis).
    2. Justin Martyr. First Apology 65–67 (ordo: Word, prayers, kiss of peace, Eucharist; participation not ordinary food).
    3. Irenaeus. Against Heresies 4.18.4–6; 5.2.2–3 (creation offered and taken into Christ; Eucharist as pledge of resurrection).
    4. Cyril of Jerusalem. Mystagogical Catecheses 5.1–10 (real participation; epiclesis and reverent approach).
    5. Basil of Caesarea. On the Holy Spirit 27 (Spirit and doxology; sanctification) and Letter 93 (tradition of Eucharistic prayer; Spirit’s role).
    6. John Chrysostom. Homilies on 1 Corinthians 27–28 (1 Cor 11: divisions condemned; Eucharist demands unity and mercy) and Homilies on Hebrews 17 (Heb 10 approach; reverence and boldness).

    Liturgical Integration: [season/feast/hymn/lectionary]
    Maundy Thursday places Luke 22 at the Church’s center: the new covenant is given as meal and command. Every Lord’s Day then becomes a small Pascha: the Church “proclaims the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26), living between cross and Parousia. The Eucharistic Prayer’s anamnesis and epiclesis enact the book’s grammar: remembrance is participatory because the Spirit is God‑active, making the once‑for‑all offering present as communion, not repeated as a parallel cult (Heb 10:10–14; Cyril, Myst. Cat. 5). The Peace, Offertory, and alms show the ethical entailment: covenant renewal requires reconciliation and mercy before communion (Matt 5:23–24; 1 Cor 11:20–22; Heb 13:16).

    Practical Outcomes: [pastoral/catechetical/ethical]
    1. Pastoral: Teach weary believers that the Eucharist is not “another demand” but a gift of participation—Christ giving Himself to form endurance and joy (John 6:53–57; Heb 10:22–25).
    2. Catechetical: Explain anamnesis as biblical remembrance: like Passover, it makes saving acts present and identity-forming (Exod 12:24–27; Luke 22:19).
    3. Ethical: Make unity and mercy explicit: divisions, contempt for the poor, and unreconciled anger contradict communion (1 Cor 11:17–22; Matt 5:23–24).
    4. Formation: Encourage a “Eucharistic week”: thanksgiving, hospitality, almsgiving, and forgiving speech as the continuation of the Table (Heb 13:16; Eph 4:32).

    Objections & Replies: [3–5 strongest]

    Chapter Body (drafted in the blueprint’s logic)

    Setup/Context: From the Incarnate Temple to the covenant meal that makes participation durable

    Chapter 16 announced that the Covenant–Temple plot becomes personal: the Word tabernacled among us, and Jesus named His body the Temple (John 1:14; 2:21). But a question immediately follows. If the temple is now Christ Himself, how do His people share in that temple‑presence after the Ascension? How does the once‑for‑all Cross become more than a remembered event?

    Jesus answers not with a new philosophy but with a meal. At Passover time He takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it, and says, “This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in anamnesis of me” (Luke 22:19). He takes the cup: “This cup… is the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:20). The covenant is enacted in a liturgical act that binds Word, gift, and command. As at Sinai (Exod 24:8–11), covenant is not merely declared; it is eaten.

    Paul later hands on the same tradition and adds its eschatological horizon: “as often as you eat… you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26). The Eucharist therefore locates the Church between the ages. It is the Church’s stable form of participation.

    Question‑at‑Issue: How can one sacrifice become ongoing participation without repetition or reduction?

    The doctrinal question is sharp: How can the Church truly participate in the once‑for‑all sacrifice without implying re‑sacrifice? Hebrews insists Christ offered Himself once for all (Heb 10:10–14). Yet Paul insists the cup and bread are “participation” (koinōnia) in Christ’s blood and body (1 Cor 10:16). The Church cannot satisfy Scripture by reducing communion to mental recollection; nor can she satisfy Scripture by implying multiple offerings.

    The chapter’s burden is to show that Scripture already has a category for this: anamnesis. In the biblical imagination, remembrance is not mere psychological recall but covenant participation—Passover makes Israel present to the saving act that formed them (Exod 12:24–27). Christ’s “Do this” extends that logic to the new covenant. The mechanism is not human imagination but the Spirit’s God-active work: the Spirit actualizes what Christ instituted, making the once‑for‑all gift present as communion.

    Prior Answers (why insufficient): three ways to miss anamnesis

    1. Eucharist as bare symbol/memory. This fails Paul’s language. The cup is not a symbol about participation but “participation” itself (1 Cor 10:16). It also fails Exodus 24’s covenant logic, where words and blood lead to eating in God’s presence (Exod 24:8–11). Biblical covenant meals enact reality; they do not merely illustrate it.

    2. Eucharist as re‑sacrifice (a parallel cult). Hebrews excludes this: Christ’s offering is once for all (Heb 10:10–14). The Eucharist does not add another offering; it participates in the one offering made in history and now living in the heavenly sanctuary (Heb 9:11–15). The Church’s liturgy is not repetition but communion.

    3. Eucharist as private spirituality. Paul’s rebuke in 1 Corinthians 11 shows the opposite: the meal is ecclesial and ethical. Divisions and contempt for the poor contradict the Table (1 Cor 11:17–22). The Eucharist forms a people; it is not a private moment.

    Each answer collapses the Covenant–Temple fabric: either removing reality, multiplying sacrifice, or severing worship from love.

    Scripture/Event Anchor: institution and apostolic tradition—anamnesis as covenant participation

    Luke 22:19–20 anchors the chapter: body “given for you,” cup “new covenant,” command “do this,” and the key term anamnesis. The words bind sacrifice and meal, indicating that the death Jesus is about to die will be given to His people as communion.

    1 Corinthians 11:23–26 provides the apostolic frame. Paul “received” and “delivered” this tradition (1 Cor 11:23), locating it at the center of the Church’s identity. The Eucharist is proclamation: the Church announces the Lord’s death not only verbally but sacramentally—by eating and drinking in a way that makes the Cross present as covenant renewal. Its horizon is eschatological: “until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26). The Table is the Church’s weekly dwelling in the already/not‑yet.

    To keep the Covenant–Temple pattern coherent, we add Paul’s explicit term: koinōnia—participation (1 Cor 10:16–17). The many become one body because they share one bread (1 Cor 10:17). This is temple logic: the people become the dwelling by sharing the sacrifice‑gift.

    Patristic Turn: early Christian witness—anamnesis, epiclesis, and ethical entailment

    The Fathers confirm that the earliest Church understood the Eucharist as participatory remembrance, Spirit‑effected, ethically demanding.

    The Didache presents Eucharist as thanksgiving and unity: the scattered are gathered into one, and the Lord’s Day assembly is integral (Did. 9–10; 14). The tone is covenantal and communal, not merely symbolic.

    Justin Martyr describes a recognizable ordo—readings, prayers, peace, Eucharist—and insists the gifts are not “common bread and common drink” but participation in Christ’s flesh and blood (1 Apol. 65–67). The point is realism without parallel cult: participation in what Christ instituted.

    Irenaeus grounds Eucharistic realism in creation and resurrection. Bread and wine—creation’s gifts—are offered and become communion, pledging the resurrection of the body (AH 4.18; 5.2). This binds temple logic to eschatology: the Table is foretaste of new creation.

    Cyril of Jerusalem articulates reverent approach and the Spirit’s sanctifying action. In his mystagogical instruction, the epiclesis is not decorative: it names the Spirit’s God-active work, making participation real and transformative (Myst. Cat. 5). Communion trains holiness.

    Basil provides the doctrinal ballast: the Spirit sanctifies and constitutes worship, and the Church’s prayer invokes the Spirit’s action in fidelity to Scripture’s promise of participation (On the Holy Spirit 27). Basil’s concern matches Hebrews: one sacrifice, made present as communion by God’s action.

    Chrysostom drives the ethical edge. He reads 1 Corinthians 11 as a warning that Eucharist without mercy is contradiction: divisions and neglect of the poor profane the Table (1 Cor 11:17–22). Eucharistic participation must become Eucharistic ethics.

    The patristic witness therefore converges on the chapter’s claim: anamnesis is participatory, epiclesis is the Spirit’s God-active actualization, and the Table demands unity and mercy.

    Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis): anamnesis and epiclesis—participation in the once‑for‑all offering

    1. Anamnesis is covenant remembrance as participation. As Passover re‑presents deliverance to form identity (Exod 12:24–27), so the Eucharist re‑presents Christ’s once‑for‑all sacrifice to form the Church. “Do this” is not merely “think about this.” It is “enter this gift.”

    2. The Eucharist participates in the once‑for‑all offering; it does not repeat it. Hebrews insists finality (Heb 10:10–14). The Eucharist is the Church’s participation in that final offering because Christ lives, reigns, and intercedes in the true sanctuary (Heb 9:11–15).

    3. Epiclesis names the Spirit’s God-active work of actualization. The Church does not make Christ present by intensity of feeling; the Spirit makes communion real by divine agency (2 Cor 3:16–18). This safeguards realism without a parallel cult: God acts; we receive.

    4. The Table forms one Body‑Temple and yields covenantal love. Paul’s logic is ecclesial: “Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body” (1 Cor 10:17). Therefore the Eucharist is covenant renewal that necessarily issues in mercy, reconciliation, and self‑offering (Heb 13:16; Rom 12:1).

    This synthesis maintains the book’s locked grammar: Christ is the living Mercy Seat; the Spirit is God-active; the ordo forms the nous; telos is covenantal love; and worship is eschatological participation.

    Payoff/Implications (worship/ethics/formation): Eucharistic life as “liturgy after the liturgy”

    Worship: The Eucharist is the Church’s stable participation in Christ’s once‑for‑all offering. It is the weekly return from exile into communion: confession, peace, thanksgiving, and communion enact covenant renewal (Heb 10:22–25). The Table is not a supplement to preaching; it is covenant enacted.

    Ethics: Paul’s warning must be heard as mercy. Communion requires discernment and reconciliation; contempt for the poor profanes the Table (1 Cor 11:20–22, 28–32). Eucharistic participation therefore requires Eucharistic mercy: almsgiving, hospitality, and truthful speech are not “nice additions”; they are the moral shape of communion (Heb 13:16; Eph 4:32).

    Formation: The Eucharist trains the nous in gratitude and hope. Because the Church proclaims the Lord’s death “until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26), the Table becomes a school of patience and expectation. The Spirit is God-active, forming fruit over time: forgiveness becomes possible because communion reorders the heart; generosity becomes natural because thanksgiving reshapes desire.

    Part IV will now press the logic of access. If anamnesis makes participation durable, Hebrews will explain how access is opened: through the veil—His flesh—so all may draw near (Heb 10:19–22). The Table and the torn veil belong to one movement.

    Objections & Replies: [3–5 strongest]

    1. Objection: “Does anamnesis just mean ‘remember mentally’?”
      Reply: In Scripture, remembrance is covenantal and enacted. Passover remembrance forms identity by participating in the saving event (Exod 12:24–27). Paul’s language of proclamation by eating and drinking (1 Cor 11:26) and participation (1 Cor 10:16) confirms that the Eucharist is more than cognition.

    2. Objection: “Does Eucharistic realism imply repeating Christ’s sacrifice?”
      Reply: Hebrews explicitly denies repetition: Christ offered once for all (Heb 10:10–14). The Eucharist participates in that completed offering because Christ lives and ministers in the true sanctuary (Heb 9:11–15). Participation is communion in the one sacrifice, not a parallel oblation.

    3. Objection: “Isn’t this too ‘liturgical’—what about faith alone?”
      Reply: Paul binds faith and sacramental participation: the cup and bread are koinōnia (1 Cor 10:16), received in faith, and judged when approached in unbelief and contempt (1 Cor 11:29). The Eucharist is not an alternative to faith; it is faith’s covenantal mode of receiving Christ’s gift within the Church’s ordo (Acts 2:42).

    4. Objection: “Why insist on ethics (unity, mercy) in a chapter about worship?”
      Reply: Because Scripture insists. Paul’s primary correction in 1 Corinthians 11 is ethical: divisions and neglect of the poor contradict the Lord’s Supper (1 Cor 11:17–22). Hebrews likewise joins approach with mutual exhortation and doing good (Heb 10:24–25; 13:16). Covenant renewal necessarily issues in covenantal love.

    5. Objection: “What if someone doesn’t ‘feel’ anything at communion—does participation fail?”
      Reply: Participation depends on Christ’s promise and the Spirit’s God-active work, not on emotional intensity (Luke 22:19–20; 1 Cor 10:16). The fruit may be quiet and slow: endurance, repentance, reconciliation, and thanksgiving over time (Gal 5:22–25). The criterion is not sensation but faithfulness and transformation.
