    Chapter 3 — Biblical anthropology, nature and person, the image of God, union with Christ, and the nous

    Thesis: Biblical anthropology, illumined by the distinction of person and nature revealed in Christ, discloses humanity as created in the image of God, wounded by sin, and renewed through union with Christ by the Spirit. This chapter argues that (1) the gospel includes both the objective healing of human nature in the incarnate Son and (2) the personal transformation of each hypostasis by the Holy Spirit, especially through the renewal of the nous within the ecclesial Ordo (Word–Table–Prayer). The Spirit’s renewal is enacted as a training of attention that re-forms desire, so that the image may advance toward its eschatological telos of divine likeness.

    Biblical Ground (passages): Genesis 1:26–27; Luke 24:45; Romans 12:2; 2 Corinthians 3:18; Colossians 3:10; John 1:14. Deut 6:4–9; Ps 19:7–11; Prov 4:20–27; Jer 31:31–34; Ezek 36:26–27; Matt 6:22–23; Matt 22:37; Mark 2:6–8; Mark 7:20–23; Mark 12 :29–34; Luke 10:27; Luke 24:13–35, 45; John 15:1–10; Rom 8:5–9; Rom 12:1–2; 1 Cor 1:10; 1 Cor 2:10–16; 2 Cor 3:14–18; Gal 2:19 –20; Eph 1:17–18; Eph 2:1–10; Eph 4:17–24; Phil 2:1–11; Col 3:1–17; 2 Pet 1:3–4; Jas 1:22–25; Heb 5:12–14.

    One‑liners for each move (keep it tight)

    • Setup/Context: Fragmented modern accounts of “the self” (psychological, biological, or voluntarist) obscure the gospel’s anthropological depth.

    • Question-at-Issue: What is the human person in light of Christ—one person in two natures—and the Spirit’s work of conforming us to him?

    • Prior Answers: Reduction to interior experience or moral performance; neglect of ecclesial, sacramental, and eschatological dimensions.

    • Scripture/Event Anchor: Creation in God’s image (Gen 1:26–27); renewal in Christ (Col 3:10).

    • Patristic Turn: Maximus on nous–logos–pneuma; Athanasius on deification; Gregory of Nyssa on image and likeness.

    • Doctrinal Resolution: Chalcedonian clarification of person/nature as a key for a non-reductionist anthropology.

    • Payoff/Implications: Discipleship as participatory transformation—renewal of the nous through liturgy, Scripture, and the Spirit’s epiclesis—so that attention is re-aimed, desire re-formed, and persons come to share in Christ’s healed humanity.

    1. Biblical Anthropology and the Question of the Human Person

    Scripture begins with a claim both simple and inexhaustible: “Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness” (Gen 1:26). Here biblical anthropology is immediately relational and vocational. Humanity is addressed by God and oriented to communion; the human being is neither an isolated self nor merely a biological organism, but a living hypostasis of human nature called to know God, bear his rule, and image his life.

    Yet the fall reveals a second truth: sin does not unmake the human creature, but disorders it. Human nature remains human; the image remains real; but the path to likeness becomes obstructed. The result is a fractured integrity—misdirected desire, darkened perception, and a loss of fitting conformity to God. This is why the Church’s catechesis cannot begin with “How do I feel?” but with “Who am I before God, and what am I for?”

    2. Person and Nature: Christological Key

    The Council of Chalcedon (451) confesses Christ as one divine person (hypostasis) in two natures (physeis), “without confusion, without change, without division, without separation.” This distinction clarifies that nature answers what something is, while person answers who.

    Applied to humanity, this yields two crucial guardrails: 1. Against reductionism: We do not reduce persons to functions (psychology), to bodies (biology), or to wills (voluntarism). A person is irreducible. 2. Against abstraction: We do not treat “person” as a disembodied ghost. Persons are not floating selves; they subsist as embodied, ensouled human nature.

    Christ is the key because in him the personal “who” (the eternal Son) truly assumes the human “what” (our nature) without diminishing either. Therefore, the gospel is not the rescue of souls from bodies, but the healing and glorification of humanity in the Son.

    3. The Image of God and Its Wounding

    Genesis’ “image and likeness” invites careful interpretation. Patristic tradition frequently reads “image” as a given structural gift and “likeness” as a dynamic telos—an appointed maturation in communion with God. One influential strand (especially in Maximus) discerns within the image a triadic pattern corresponding to a Trinitarian anthropology: nous, logos, and pneuma—spiritual perception (the “eye of the heart”), rational articulation and meaning-making, and vital/spiritual dynamism.

    Sin, then, is not merely rule-breaking but a misorientation of the whole human life: - the nous becomes clouded—perception of God is dulled and redirected toward idols; - the logos becomes distorted—speech and reason are pressed into self-justification and fragmentation; - the pneuma becomes dissipated—vitality is driven by passions rather than the Spirit’s life.

    This diagnosis also helps the Church name the pastoral crisis: without a sound biblical anthropology, the gospel is reduced to moralism (“try harder”) or therapy (“feel better”), and discipleship becomes technique rather than participation.

    4. The Incarnation and the Renewal of Nature

    “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). In the incarnation the Son assumes our nature to heal it from within. He does not bypass human life; he lives it—obedient, faithful, and fully oriented to the Father. Through his life, death, and resurrection, Christ manifests human nature as it is meant to be: not autonomous, but filial; not curved inward, but eucharistic; not subject to death, but opened to incorruptibility.

    Here it is helpful to distinguish objective and personal dimensions of salvation: - Objective (in Christ): human nature is healed, clarified, and perfected in the incarnate economy. - Personal (in us): each human hypostasis is united to Christ and transformed by the Spirit.

    Athanasius summarizes the horizon: “God became man that man might become god” (On the Incarnation 54). Deification is not absorption into deity, but participation in God’s life through Christ’s restored humanity.

    5. Union with Christ and the Work of the Spirit

    Salvation is not exhausted in Christ’s objective accomplishment; it is enacted in us by the Holy Spirit. The Spirit unites our wounded image to Christ’s perfected/glorified humanity—real participation, not metaphor.

    The point of contact is especially the nous. Scripture repeatedly relate salvation to illumination and transformation of perception: - “Then he opened their minds (nous) to understand the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45). - “Be transformed by the renewal of your mind (nous)” (Rom 12:2).

    This is not a privileging of intellect over body. The nous is not merely discursive reasoning (dianoia), but the deep faculty of spiritual apprehension by which the whole person is oriented.

    Attention as the hinge between illumination and desire. The Spirit’s renewal of the nous ordinarily takes the form of a trained attentiveness. What the nous repeatedly attends to becomes what the heart comes to want. Attention is therefore never neutral: it is already a moral and spiritual orientation. When attention is scattered, desire is commandeered by fear, novelty, and passion; when attention is gathered in Christ, desire is slowly re-formed into freedom. Thus, as the Spirit illumines the nous, the logos is re-ordered toward truthful confession and wise action, and the pneuma is vivified toward love, endurance, and worship. In this way, nature is renewed in Christ, and persons are progressively conformed to Christ—Christlikeness as the Spirit’s work.

    6. Liturgical and Ecclesial Mediation

    The Church’s worship is not ornament but ontology. The liturgy is the Spirit’s school of personhood.

    In the Eucharist, anamnesis is not mere recollection but participation in the saving economy made present. In epiclesis, the Church calls upon the Spirit to make us sharers in Christ—receiving not simply “benefits,” but communion.

    The Ordo—Word, Table, Prayer forms a pedagogy of desire by first training attention: - Word gathers the nous—teaching it where to look and how to see reality in God’s light; - Table concentrates attention upon Christ’s self-giving presence—feeding communion and redirecting desire from possession to participation; - Prayer sustains and purifies attention over time—integrating the whole person into doxology, repentance, and intercession.

    Here discipleship becomes participatory rather than performative: the Church learns not only what to do, but what to be—humanity in Christ.

    7. Practical Outcomes

    • Catechesis: Teach the person/nature distinction as a key to identity and vocation—who we are before we ask what we must do.
    • Spiritual Formation: Emphasize the renewal of the nous through Scripture, sacrament, and prayer; distinguish nous from mere technique or information.
    • Discipleship: Recover the training of attention as central to formation. Teach believers not only what to believe or do, but how to attend—to Scripture, to Christ’s presence, to neighbor—so that desire may be slowly re-aligned with the Kingdom.
    • Pastoral Care: Address reductionist views of the self (moralistic, therapeutic, or materialist) with a vision of healed nature and transformed personhood.
    • Mission: Witness to a gospel that restores not only behavior but being—an anthropology of participation rather than self-construction.

    8. Objections & Replies

    • Objection: Does focusing on the nous privilege intellect over the body? Reply: No. The nous is the “eye of the heart,” the integrative faculty by which the embodied person is oriented toward God; its renewal re-orders desire, practice, and communal life.
    • Objection: Does the language of “training attention” introduce technique and moralism? Reply: Not if attention is framed as the Spirit-enabled posture of receptivity and fidelity. The Church trains attention by giving the nous a true object (Christ) and a true rhythm (Word–Table–Prayer), not by supplying self-improvement hacks.
    • Objection: Does this diminish grace by stressing human faculties? Reply: No. Grace precedes, empowers, and completes. The renewal of nous–logos–pneuma—including the healing of attention and desire—is not a human project but the fruit of divine initiative enacted through Word, sacrament, and the Spirit.
