    Chapter 3: Biblical anthropology, nature and person, the image of God, union with Christ, and the nous

    Thesis

    Biblical anthropology, illumined by the Christological distinction between person (hypostasis) and nature (physis), discloses humanity as created in the image of God, wounded by sin, and renewed through union with Christ by the Spirit. This chapter argues that (1) the gospel includes both the objective healing and glorification of human nature in the incarnate Son and (2) the personal transformation of each human hypostasis by the Holy Spirit, especially through the renewal of the nous within the ecclesial Ordo of Word–Table–Prayer. The Spirit’s renewal is enacted as a training of attention that re‑forms desire, so that the image may advance toward its eschatological telos of divine likeness.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    • Genesis 1:26–27
    • John 1:14
    • Luke 24:45
    • Romans 12:2
    • 2 Corinthians 3:18
    • Colossians 3:10

    In Genesis 1:26–27, humanity is created “in our image, according to our likeness,” establishing a relational and vocational anthropology in which human beings are addressed by God and entrusted with participation in his rule. John 1:14 reveals the incarnate Son as the one who assumes human nature itself, so that our being may be healed and glorified in him. Luke 24:45 and Romans 12:2 link salvation to the renewal of the nous—the “mind” or spiritual perception—while 2 Corinthians 3:18 and Colossians 3:10 depict believers as being transformed into Christ’s image and clothed with a renewed humanity. Together these texts ground an account of the image as wounded yet real, and of salvation as participatory renewal in Christ through the Spirit.

    Chapter Body

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    Modern accounts of “the self” are often fragmented and reductive. In many cultural and pastoral settings, the human person is implicitly treated either as a psychological interior (a bundle of experiences and feelings), as a biological organism (a sophisticated animal whose behavior can be fully explained by brain and body), or as a voluntarist agent (a will that defines itself by its choices). Each of these partial views captures something important, but none can function as an adequate Christian anthropology.

    By contrast, Scripture begins not with self-analysis but with a divine address: “Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness” (Gen 1:26). Humanity is immediately presented as relational and vocational—called into communion with God, given a task within creation, and defined not by interior states or sheer choice but by a received identity before God. The fall does not erase this identity, but disorders it. Human nature remains human; the image remains real; yet desire is misdirected, perception is darkened, and the path from image to likeness is obstructed.

    Pastorally, this means that if the Church simply adopts the surrounding culture’s anthropology, the gospel is inevitably reduced. Without a biblically and christologically grounded vision of the human person, proclamation collapses either into moralism (“try harder, do better”) or into therapy (“feel better, cope more successfully”). Discipleship becomes a technique rather than a participation in Christ. The more urgent question, then, is not “How do I feel about myself?” but “Who am I before God, and what am I for in light of Christ and the Spirit’s work?”

    Section Summary:
    This chapter arises from the collision between fragmented modern images of the self and Scripture’s claim that humanity is addressed by God, bears his image, and is called into renewed communion in Christ. The central question is: What is the human person in light of Christ—one person in two natures—and the Spirit’s work of conforming us to him?

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: the “self” is a psychological interior, a biological mechanism, or a choosing will.
    After: the person is a hypostasis of human nature addressed by God, created in the image, and ordered to communion and likeness in Christ.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Long before explicit secular anthropologies, Christian practice itself has sometimes fallen into reductionist patterns. One common tendency is to equate the person with interior experience: “I am what I feel.” Here the goal of ministry becomes managing or optimizing emotions, and the gospel becomes a promise of inward equilibrium. Another tendency is to reduce the person to moral performance: “I am what I do.” Holiness is measured by visible compliance, and salvation is functionally framed as behavior management. A third reduction leans toward materialism: “I am my body and brain.” Human beings are treated as complex biological systems, and spiritual language becomes either metaphorical or marginal.

    Each of these impulses contains a partial truth: feelings matter; actions matter; embodiment matters. Yet taken as governing frames, they cannot sustain a properly Christian account of salvation. If the self is exhausted by feeling or performance, then salvation will be imagined merely as emotional relief or moral improvement. If the human being is only a biological machine, then talk of communion with God, deification, or participation in Christ becomes unintelligible. In each case, ecclesial life shrinks: worship is reduced to inspiration, ethics to technique, and pastoral care to adjustment.

    These prior answers also neglect the ecclesial, sacramental, and eschatological dimensions of the gospel. The Church becomes a support group or an ethical society, rather than the body that participates in Christ’s healed humanity and lives toward the resurrection. The sacraments are treated as symbols of our inner states rather than as means by which the Spirit unites us to Christ. The eschatological horizon of likeness to God is obscured behind immediate needs and projects.

    Section Summary:
    Psychological, moralistic, and materialist reductions each grasp something real but finally prove inadequate: they cannot sustain Scripture’s portrayal of humanity as image-bearing, sin-wounded, and ordered to participation in God through Christ and the Spirit. These frames truncate both the scope of salvation and the depth of ecclesial life.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: “salvation” is emotional relief, moral performance, or successful coping within the limits of the present age.
    After: salvation is participation in Christ’s healed humanity, within the Church, ordered toward deification and resurrection.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    Genesis 1:26–27 announces the human as created in the image and likeness of God. From the beginning, humanity is constituted by divine address, entrusted with dominion, and oriented toward a destiny of likeness. This is not a static possession but a vocation: to grow into the likeness of the God whose image we bear. Even after the fall, Scripture never says that the image is lost; rather, the image is wounded and obscured. Human life now unfolds under conditions of misdirected desire, distorted perception, and disordered rule.

    The gospel does not begin with human striving but with God’s decisive act: “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). In Jesus Christ, the eternal Son assumes our nature without ceasing to be who he is. His life, death, and resurrection manifest human nature as it is meant to be—filial, eucharistic, and oriented wholly to the Father. In him we see not an escape from created finitude, but a redeemed and glorified humanity that is the template and source of our renewal.

    Scripture ties this renewal to a transformation of perception and understanding. The risen Christ “opened their minds (nous) to understand the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45), and Paul exhorts believers: “Be transformed by the renewal of your mind (nous)” (Rom 12:2). Here the nous is not bare intellect but the deep “eye of the heart” by which the whole person attends to reality. As we behold “the glory of the Lord” we are “being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another” (2 Cor 3:18); we “have stripped off the old self with its practices and have clothed [ourselves] with the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge according to the image of its creator” (Col 3:9–10).

    Section Summary:
    Scripture presents humanity as created in God’s image and called to likeness, wounded but not erased by sin, and renewed through the incarnate Son who assumes and perfects our nature and opens the nous to God’s reality.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: the human problem is primarily behavioral or emotional, requiring better rules or better feelings.
    After: the human problem is a wounded image and darkened nous; the solution is God’s assumption and renewal of our nature in Christ, and the Spirit’s transformation of perception and desire.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The patristic tradition reads Genesis and the gospel narratives not as isolated texts but as parts of a single economy of salvation. A widespread interpretation distinguishes between “image” and “likeness”: the image is a structural gift given in creation, while likeness is an eschatological telos—the journey into mature communion with God. Gregory of Nyssa, in On the Making of Man, portrays human life as an infinite progression into divine likeness, grounded in the unlosable image.

    Within this broad consensus, one influential strand—especially in Maximus the Confessor—discerns within the image a triadic pattern corresponding to a kind of Trinitarian anthropology: nous, logos, and pneuma. The nous is the spiritual perception or “eye of the heart”; the logos is rational articulation and meaning-making; and the pneuma is the vital dynamism of the person in love and movement. Sin is thus more than rule-breaking: it is the misorientation of the whole human life—clouded nous, distorted logos, dissipated pneuma.

    Athanasius famously summarizes the incarnational horizon: “God became man that man might become god” (On the Incarnation 54). Deification (theosis) does not mean absorption into the divine essence, but participation in God’s life through the Son’s restored humanity. Augustine, Cyril of Alexandria, and John of Damascus each, in distinctive ways, emphasize that the image’s healing and elevation occur in and through Christ, within the Church, by the Spirit. Taken together, the Fathers read Scripture as revealing an anthropology ordered to deification, grounded in Christ’s person and work.

    Section Summary:
    The Fathers discern in Scripture an anthropology in which the image is retained but wounded, ordered to likeness, and structured by nous–logos–pneuma; they interpret Christ’s incarnate and deifying work as the healing and elevation of this image within the Church.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: modern habits read Scripture as a set of religious ideas about an isolated individual self.
    After: the Church’s patristic reading unveils a coherent economy in which human nous–logos–pneuma are healed and deified through union with the incarnate Son.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The Council of Chalcedon (451) confesses Christ as one divine person (hypostasis) in two natures (physeis), “without confusion, without change, without division, without separation.” This distinction clarifies that nature answers the question what something is, while person answers who. Applied to anthropology, it yields two guardrails: we must neither reduce persons to functions (psychology, biology, or sheer will) nor treat “person” as a disembodied ghost. A human person is an irreducible “who” who subsists as embodied, ensouled human nature.

    Christ is the key. In him, the eternal “who” of the Son truly assumes the human “what” of our nature without diminishing either. The gospel, then, is not the rescue of souls from bodies, nor the enhancement of a generic spirituality, but the healing and glorification of humanity in the incarnate Son. Here we can distinguish an objective and a personal dimension: objectively, human nature is healed, clarified, and perfected in the economy of the incarnate Word; personally, each human hypostasis is united to Christ and transformed by the Spirit. Deification thus has both a Christological center and a pneumatological extension.

    This doctrinal synthesis allows a non-reductionist anthropology. The person is more than psyche, body, or will, yet never exists apart from them. Human nature, assumed and renewed in Christ, is the site where salvation is enacted; persons participate in this renewed nature through union with Christ by the Spirit, especially in the Church’s sacramental and liturgical life. Chalcedonian Christology thus becomes a key for understanding both who the human person is and how salvation truly heals and elevates our being.

    Section Summary:
    By reading Scripture with the Church, we arrive at a Chalcedonian anthropology: persons are irreducible “who’s” who subsist in human nature; this nature is assumed, healed, and glorified in Christ; and each person is called to participate personally in this renewed humanity through the Spirit.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: we could not speak coherently about salvation without either collapsing the person into nature (biology, psychology, will) or dissolving nature into a vague “self.”
    After: we can confess and live an anthropology in which human nature is objectively renewed in Christ and each person is personally united to him by the Spirit.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    If salvation includes both the healing of human nature in Christ and the transformation of persons by the Spirit, then Christian formation cannot be reduced to moral effort or doctrinal information. Scripture’s language of nous points to a deeper transformation: “Then he opened their minds (nous) to understand the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45); “Be transformed by the renewal of your mind (nous)” (Rom 12:2). The nous is the faculty of spiritual perception by which the whole person attends to and receives reality in God. Its renewal therefore reorders every dimension of life.

    Attention is the hinge between illumination and desire. What the nous repeatedly attends to becomes what the heart comes to want. Attention is never neutral: scattered attention leaves desire at the mercy of fear, novelty, and passion; gathered attention trains desire toward freedom in Christ. As the Spirit illumines the nous, the logos is re-directed toward truthful confession and wise action, and the pneuma is vivified toward love, endurance, and worship. In this way, nature is renewed in Christ, and persons are progressively conformed to him.

    Spiritual disciplines, then, are best understood as practices of Spirit-enabled attentiveness rather than self-improvement techniques. Catechesis becomes an initiation into a new way of seeing—learning to perceive oneself and the world in Christ. Pastoral care addresses not only behavior or feeling but the deeper question of who the person is before God and how their nous–logos–pneuma are being drawn into Christ’s pattern of life.

    Section Summary:
    The renewal of the nous by the Spirit reorders attention, desire, and practice so that human persons may participate in Christ’s healed humanity; formation is an apprenticeship in attentiveness rather than a project of self-optimization.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: “change” is primarily a matter of willpower, technique, or therapeutic adjustment.
    After: change is Spirit-enabled participation in Christ, enacted through the renewal of the nous and the re-forming of desire.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church’s worship is not ornament but ontology: in Word, Table, and Prayer the Spirit enacts and sustains our participation in Christ’s humanity. The liturgy is the Spirit’s school of personhood, where attention is gathered, desire is re-formed, and the image is led toward likeness.

    In the Eucharist, anamnesis is more than recollection; it is participation in the saving economy made present. In epiclesis, the Church calls upon the Spirit not simply to bless bread and wine, but to make us sharers in Christ—to receive not abstractions or “benefits” only, but communion with the crucified and risen Lord. Baptismal epiclesis similarly marks personal incorporation into Christ, where the Spirit seals each hypostasis into the renewed humanity of the Son.

    The Ordo—Word, Table, Prayer forms a pedagogy of desire by training attention. The Word gathers the nous, teaching it where to look and how to see reality in God’s light. The Table concentrates attention upon Christ’s self-giving presence, redirecting desire from possession to participation. Prayer sustains and purifies attention over time, integrating the whole person into doxology, repentance, and intercession. Here discipleship becomes participatory rather than performative: the Church learns not only what to do but what to be—humanity in Christ.

    Frame Reinforcement Loop (1–2 sentences):
    The Church does not merely express doctrine in worship; she re-installs the gospel’s frame of reality week by week. In Word, Table, and Prayer, the Spirit stabilizes a Christocentric anthropology in which persons learn to see, desire, and act from within Christ’s healed humanity.

    Section Summary:
    Through baptismal incorporation, Eucharistic participation, and the Ordo of Word–Table–Prayer, the Church becomes the concrete place where the doctrine of renewed humanity is embodied, transmitted, and reinforced as the Spirit’s pedagogy of attention and desire.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: worship is a periodic expression of religious feeling or inspiration.
    After: worship is the Spirit’s pedagogy that stabilizes the gospel’s frame of reality in persons and communities, training nous–logos–pneuma for participation in Christ.

    Practical Outcomes

    • Catechesis: Teach the person/nature distinction and the image–likeness dynamic as central to Christian identity and vocation, so that believers learn who they are before God in Christ before asking what they must do.
    • Spiritual Formation: Emphasize the renewal of the nous through Scripture, sacrament, and prayer, distinguishing this deep spiritual perception from mere information transfer or technique.
    • Pastoral Care: Address reductionist views of the self—moralistic, therapeutic, or materialist—by offering a vision of healed nature and transformed personhood in which each hypostasis is called to participate in Christ’s deified humanity.
    • Mission: Bear witness to a gospel that restores not only behavior but being, offering an anthropology of participation rather than self-construction and inviting the world into communion with Christ through the life of the Church.

    Objections & Replies

    • Objection: Does focusing on the nous privilege intellect over the body and everyday life?
      Reply: No. The nous is not bare intellect but the “eye of the heart,” the integrative faculty by which the embodied person is oriented toward God. Its renewal entails the reordering of desire, practice, and communal life, not an escape into abstraction.

    • Objection: Does the language of “training attention” introduce technique and moralism into spiritual formation?
      Reply: Not if attention is framed as a Spirit-enabled posture of receptivity and fidelity. The Church trains attention by giving the nous a true object (Christ) and a true rhythm (Word–Table–Prayer), rather than by offering self-improvement hacks.

    • Objection: Does this account of nous–logos–pneuma and deification diminish grace by stressing human faculties and activity?
      Reply: No. Grace precedes, empowers, and completes. The renewal of nous–logos–pneuma—including the healing of attention and desire—is not a human project that competes with grace but the fruit of divine initiative enacted through Word, sacrament, and the Spirit’s continual work.

    • Anatolios, Khaled. Retrieving Nicaea: The Development and Meaning of Trinitarian Doctrine. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2011.
      A historical and theological retrieval of Nicene and pro-Nicene Trinitarianism that clarifies person–nature language and the economy of salvation, providing crucial background for a Chalcedonian anthropology.

    • Maximus the Confessor. On the Cosmic Mystery of Jesus Christ. Translated by Paul M. Blowers and Robert Louis Wilken. Crestwood, NY: St. Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 2003.
      A collection of Maximus’s key texts in translation, central for understanding his nous–logos–pneuma anthropology, his account of deification, and the Christological basis for the healing of human nature.

    • Lossky, Vladimir. The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church. Crestwood, NY: St. Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1997.
      A classic synthesis of Eastern Christian theology that develops the themes of image and likeness, the distinction between person and nature, and the doctrine of deification as the horizon of Christian anthropology.

    • Gregory of Nyssa. On the Making of Man. In Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series 2, Vol. 5.
      A foundational patristic work on creation, the image of God, and human destiny, articulating the dynamic movement from image to likeness and the open-ended growth of the soul toward God.

    • Athanasius. On the Incarnation. Translated by John Behr. Yonkers, NY: St. Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 2011.
      A seminal text on the incarnation and deification, providing the classic formula “God became man that man might become god,” and grounding anthropology in the Son’s assumption and restoration of human nature.
