    Excursus — The Handle of Grace: Attention and Glad Willing (in a Maximian Key)

    Purpose. Chapter 3 argues that the Spirit renews the nous so that persons participate in Christ’s healed humanity within the Church’s Ordo. This excursus supplies a missing hinge: how that renewal becomes stable, concrete obedience—not by moralistic effort, but by the Spirit’s healing of the mode of human willing through attention.

    Biblical Ground

    • Luke 22:42 — Christ’s human willing: “not my will, but yours be done.”
    • Luke 24:45 — the risen Christ opens the disciples’ nous to understand Scripture.
    • Romans 12:1–2 — bodily offering → renewed mind (nous) → discernment and loving of God’s will.
    • Philippians 2:12–13 — God works in us “to will and to work” (to thelein kai to energein).

    These texts name one reality: God does not save us by bypassing human willing; he saves by healing it, so that our offering becomes discernment and our discernment becomes glad obedience.

    1. Maximus’s diagnostic: the problem is not “will” but the gnomic mode of willing

    Maximus distinguishes what belongs to human nature from the wounded way persons enact that nature.

    • The natural will is the God-given inclination of human nature toward the good—toward God as our end.
    • The gnomic will names the hypostatic mode of willing under conditions of ignorance and fracture: deliberation, vacillation, and self-protective choosing. It is not “freedom” at its best, but freedom as disordered: a will that struggles to see the good clearly and therefore hesitates, bargains, or grasps.

    This is why the spiritual problem is deeper than “I sometimes choose badly.” It is that the person’s way of choosing has become unstable. The will does not move with simplicity toward God; it sways between apparent goods, and it often experiences God’s will as alien—an external demand rather than life.

    Maximus helps us name the deeper disorder: not the existence of human willing, but the gnomic instability by which persons, under sin, deliberate and choose without clear vision of the good.

    2. Christ heals human willing from within: Gethsemane as the center of ascetical realism

    Maximus’s decisive Christological claim is that the incarnate Son assumes a complete human nature, including a real human will. Salvation is not achieved by suppressing the human will, but by healing and re-ordering it in Christ’s own hypostasis.

    In Gethsemane, Christ’s human willing is fully present—he does not “play act” fear or pain. Yet his willing is not gnomic wavering that pits “my will” against the Father as rival goods. Rather, his human will is enacted in filial obedience: “not my will, but yours be done” (Luke 22:42). In Maximus’s idiom, Christ’s humanity is not saved by escaping human willing, but by willing humanly without fracture, in perfect consonance with the Father.

    This matters pastorally: the Christian life is not stoic resignation and not raw voluntarism. It is participation in the Son’s own consent, so that God’s will becomes not only commanded but desired—experienced as “good and acceptable and perfect” (Rom 12:2).

    Gethsemane is not merely a moral example; it is the Christological healing of human willing, the ground by which the Spirit can conform our wavering wills to the Son’s filial consent.

    3. Where attention fits: the battleground of logismoi becomes the hinge of willing

    Maximus is unsentimental about the mechanics of temptation and growth: the struggle is often mediated through logismoi—thoughts that propose an apparent good, interpret a situation, and invite consent.

    Here the hinge appears. Between the arrival of a thought and the act of consent lies attention:

    1. A logismos presents an apparent good (“this will give life,” “this will protect you,” “this will justify you”).
    2. Attention lingers, turns the proposal over, gives it weight—or refuses it.
    3. The gnomic will begins to deliberate: weighing, imagining, bargaining, rehearsing outcomes.
    4. Consent follows, and desire hardens into habit.

    In other words, attention is not a neutral mental spotlight. It is the creaturely place where the person either (a) grants an idol a hearing long enough for desire to attach, or (b) turns toward Christ so that the proposal is judged in the light of the true Good.

    This is why attention can be called a “handle”—not because it gives the creature control over grace, but because it is the site of receptivity where the Spirit’s illumination becomes practical. The Spirit does not heal us by bypassing thought and will, but by gathering them: opening the nous (Luke 24:45), renewing the mind (Rom 12:2), and re-forming the act of consent into Christlike obedience.

    Attention is the hinge between thought and consent: it is where gnomic deliberation either deepens toward captivity or is gathered toward Christ for discernment and freedom.

    4. The Ordo as the Spirit’s concrete therapy for gnomic willing (Word–Table–Prayer)

    Chapter 3 already argues that the Ordo forms the person; here we can state the more specific Maximian point: the Ordo is where the Spirit steadily heals the mode of willing by re-training what the person attends to, loves, and consents to.

    Word — Christ opens the nous and re-names the good

    In the Word, Christ continues the Emmaus act: he opens the nous to understand Scripture (Luke 24:45). This is not mere instruction; it is re-perception. The Word interrupts gnomic confusion by naming true goods, exposing counterfeit promises, and training discernment so that God’s will can be recognized as life.

    Table — communion re-forms consent from grasping to receiving

    At the Table, the Church does not merely recall a past event; she is given communion that judges and heals the heart’s instincts. The Eucharist trains the person away from the gnomic reflex of self-securing grasp and into the posture of filial receiving: “life is gift; God is trustworthy; I am not my own.”

    Prayer — the will is exercised as consent, not as anxious control

    In prayer, the person learns to bring desire into the light without making desire sovereign. Prayer is the repeated school of “not my will,” where attention is returned to God, and willing is slowly transfigured from resistance into consent. Over time, prayer becomes the arena where Phil 2:13 is experienced as reality: God works in us both to will and to work.

    Word, Table, and Prayer do not merely “inspire” obedience; they steadily heal the gnomic mode of willing by re-forming attention, re-naming the good, and habituating consent as participation in Christ.

    5. A practical Maximian sequence (without technique): offer → attend → discern → consent

    Romans 12:1–2 gives a simple, non-moralistic grammar for daily life that aligns with Maximus’s realism:

    • Offer: present the body as living sacrifice (Rom 12:1)—place the self under God before the day’s logismoi arrive.
    • Attend: notice what claims the heart’s gaze; refuse prolonged audience to counterfeit goods.
    • Discern: let the renewed mind “prove” what God’s will is (Rom 12:2), not abstractly, but in the concrete time-space-roles situation.
    • Consent: enact “not my will” as participation in Christ, trusting that God’s will is good—and that God supplies what he commands (Phil 2:13).

    This is not a technique for self-mastery. It is a rule of receptivity: the Spirit heals the hypostatic mode of willing by gathering attention toward Christ, so that desire is reordered and consent becomes glad.

    Attention is the handle of grace because it is where the Spirit turns gnomic wavering into filial consent—so that “not my will” becomes, over time, a willing “Amen.”

    • Ambigua (Logos–logoi; healing of perception/desire)
    • Mystagogy (ecclesial/liturgical formation as participation in the mystery of Christ)
