    Chapter 22 — Leviticus 1–9 Fulfilled: From Altar to Table — Christ, Church, Body‑Temple

    Thesis (≤2 sentences):
    Christ fulfills the sacrificial system of Leviticus, and the Holy Spirit incorporates the Church into Christ’s once‑for‑all self‑offering so that our Eucharistic anamnesis becomes real communion and our daily obedience becomes “living sacrifice.” The Church thus becomes the Body‑Temple whose priestly life manifests the kingdom as mercy, justice, and cruciform love (Heb 9–10; Rom 12:1–2; 1 Cor 10:16–17).

    Biblical Ground (passages):
    Lev 1–7; Lev 8–9; Isa 53:4–12; Mark 10:45; John 1:29; Rom 3:21–26; Rom 8:1–4; Rom 12:1–2; 1 Cor 5:7–8; 1 Cor 10:16–17; 1 Cor 11:23–29; 2 Cor 5:14–21; Eph 5:1–2; Heb 7:23–28; Heb 9:11–14, 23–28; Heb 10:1–18; 1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 5:6–10; Rev 19:6–8.

    Argument Map (Wilken‑calibrated):
    [Setup/Context → Question‑at‑Issue → Prior Answers (insufficient) → Scripture/Event Anchor → Patristic Turn → Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis) → Payoff/Implications]

    One‑liners for each move (keep it tight)

    • Setup/Context: Leviticus 1–9 gives Israel a liturgical grammar of altar, blood, washing, and communion; Part VI asks how this grammar is fulfilled when the Spirit forms the Church as Covenant–Temple.
    • Question‑at‑Issue: If Christ is the once‑for‑all sacrifice, in what sense can the Church speak of “sacrifice,” Eucharistic anamnesis, and “living sacrifice” without re‑sacrificing Christ or reducing worship to metaphor?
    • Prior Answers (why insufficient): (1) “Sacrifice is abolished; only ethics remain” (ignores Eucharistic and priestly language); (2) “The Eucharist repeats the sacrifice” (contradicts Heb 9–10); (3) “Sacrifice is only inward feeling” (fails Rom 12’s embodied offering and 1 Cor 10’s communion realism).
    • Scripture/Event Anchor: Hebrews interprets Leviticus as shadow fulfilled in Christ’s heavenly priesthood and once‑for‑all offering (Heb 9–10). Paul then names the Church’s embodied life as “living sacrifice” (Rom 12:1–2) grounded in Eucharistic participation in Christ’s body and blood (1 Cor 10:16–17).
    • Patristic Turn: The Fathers (especially Augustine, Irenaeus, Athanasius, Cyril, Chrysostom, and Maximus) read sacrifice as recapitulated in Christ and participated in ecclesially: Eucharist makes present the one offering; the Church becomes what she receives and is sent to live sacrificial love.
    • Doctrinal Resolution: The Church’s “sacrifice” is participatory, not competitive: by epiclesis the Spirit makes the Church’s anamnesis effective communion in the once‑for‑all sacrifice, and this sacramental participation forms a life of self‑offering mercy.
    • Payoff/Implications: Worship becomes covenant renewal at the Table; ethics becomes altar‑logic translated into daily mercy; formation retrains the nous so the Body‑Temple lives as royal‑priestly witness.

    Patristic Witness (top 6 citations):
    1. Augustine of Hippo. De civitate Dei (City of God) X (the Church as “true sacrifice” in Christ).
    2. Irenaeus of Lyons. Adversus haereses (Against Heresies) IV (recapitulation; Eucharist and creation offered in Christ).
    3. Athanasius of Alexandria. De incarnatione (On the Incarnation) (Christ’s self‑offering and renewal of humanity/temple).
    4. Cyril of Jerusalem. Mystagogical Catecheses (Eucharistic realism; epiclesis‑logic for participation).
    5. John Chrysostom. Homilies on Hebrews and Homilies on 1 Corinthians (once‑for‑all sacrifice; Eucharist and ethical demand for mercy).
    6. Maximus the Confessor. Mystagogia (liturgy forms the cosmos and the nous; sacrificial participation as deifying love).

    Liturgical Integration:
    Pascha as the horizon of all sacrifice: “Christ our Passover has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7). Eucharist as covenant renewal: the Table as peace‑offering communion fulfilled (Luke 22:19–20; 1 Cor 10:16–17). Offertory/almsgiving as translation of altar‑logic into mercy (Heb 13:15–16).

    Practical Outcomes:
    Pastoral: catechize the Church out of both guilt‑driven moralism and passive quietism by locating obedience in sacrificial participation (Rom 12:1–2; Phil 2:12–13).
    Catechetical: teach sacrifice as fulfilled and transformed: from repeated offerings to once‑for‑all offering made present by anamnesis and epiclesis (Heb 9–10; Luke 22:19).
    Ethical: form a Eucharistic people whose mercy is the “liturgy after the liturgy” (Heb 13:16; Matt 25:31–46).

    Setup/Context: Leviticus’ Altar‑Grammar and the Church’s Question

    Leviticus 1–9 is not merely ancient ritual; it is God’s pedagogical grammar for a people learning to live near divine holiness. Burnt offerings, sin offerings, and peace offerings are not interchangeable religious acts; they articulate a covenantal order: approach, cleansing, communion, and consecration (Lev 1–7; 8–9). Blood signifies life given and life returned; washing signifies readiness for nearness; the altar signifies the boundary where death is borne so that life may be shared (Lev 17:11; cf. Lev 8:6). Israel is trained to confess that communion with God is gift, not entitlement, and that approach requires mercy.

    Yet the same Torah insists that this grammar is provisional. The sacrifices repeat, and repetition itself becomes a sign: what is repeated is not yet final. Hebrews will later name this: “the law has only a shadow of the good things to come” (Heb 10:1). The question of Part VI is therefore not whether Leviticus matters, but how it matters when Christ has come and the Spirit has formed the Church as Covenant–Temple (Acts 2; 1 Cor 3:16–17; 1 Pet 2:5, 9).

    If the Church is the Spirit‑indwelt temple, does she still have “altar‑logic”? If the Table is central, is it merely a memorial meal, or is it the fulfillment of sacrificial communion? And if the Church speaks of offering herself, is this moral metaphor, or a genuine participation in Christ’s once‑for‑all sacrifice?

    Question‑at‑Issue: What Does “Sacrifice” Mean After the Once‑for‑All Offering?

    Hebrews proclaims that Christ offered himself “once for all” (Heb 9:26–28; 10:10). Yet the New Testament does not abandon sacrificial language; it transfigures it. Paul exhorts the Church to present our bodies as “a living sacrifice” (Rom 12:1). Peter calls the Church a “holy priesthood” to offer “spiritual sacrifices” (1 Pet 2:5). Hebrews itself speaks of “a sacrifice of praise” and of doing good and sharing as pleasing sacrifices (Heb 13:15–16).

    The doctrinal puzzle is therefore precise: how can sacrifice remain Christian speech without becoming either (a) denial of Christ’s sufficiency or (b) a purely inward metaphor detached from liturgy and body? How can Eucharistic anamnesis be a real making‑present of the one sacrifice without implying a parallel cult? And how does the Spirit’s epiclesis mediate this participation so that it forms the nous and the Body‑Temple into covenantal love?

    Prior Answers (Insufficient): Three Mistakes the Church Must Refuse

    1) “Sacrifice is abolished; only ethics remain.”

    This view treats sacrifice as a canceled category. But Scripture retains the category while relocating it in Christ and the Church’s participation. Rom 12:1–2 is not optional poetry; it is apostolic command grounded in the mercies of God (Rom 12:1). Likewise, 1 Pet 2:5 does not retreat from priesthood; it declares its fulfillment in the Spirit‑formed temple (1 Pet 2:5, 9). If we remove sacrifice language, we lose the grammar by which Scripture describes worship’s shape and ethics’ origin.

    2) “The Eucharist repeats Christ’s sacrifice.”

    This error tries to secure realism by multiplying offerings. Hebrews directly contradicts it: Christ does not “offer himself repeatedly” (Heb 9:25). His single offering perfects those being sanctified (Heb 10:14). Any account of Eucharistic realism must therefore be sacramental participation in the once‑for‑all offering, not a second offering beside it.

    3) “Sacrifice is only inward feeling.”

    A third reduction spiritualizes sacrifice into private intention. But the New Testament is bodily and ecclesial: “present your bodies” (Rom 12:1); “because there is one bread… we are one body” (1 Cor 10:17). The Church’s sacrifice is not a mood; it is an enacted life—worship, mercy, reconciliation, and concrete sharing (Heb 13:16).

    Scripture/Event Anchor: Hebrews and Paul as One Grammar of Fulfillment

    1) Christ the fulfillment of sacrifice: Leviticus read through Hebrews (Lev 1–7; Heb 9–10)

    Leviticus teaches a people to approach God through mediated offerings (Lev 1–7). Hebrews does not mock this; it interprets it. The sacrifices are “a shadow” because they cannot, by repetition, bring perfection (Heb 10:1–4). Their repetition becomes prophetic: it dramatizes the need for a final priest and a final offering.

    Hebrews then names Christ as both priest and offering. He enters “the greater and more perfect tent” (Heb 9:11), and he offers his own blood to cleanse conscience and inaugurate new covenant access (Heb 9:12–14; 10:19–22). The result is not abolition of nearness, but its accomplishment: “where there is forgiveness… there is no longer any offering for sin” (Heb 10:18). The altar‑logic is fulfilled, not erased: cleansing is secured; access is opened.

    The Covenant–Temple pattern reaches its center here: the Mercy Seat reality (justice and mercy meeting) is fulfilled in Christ’s self‑offering (Rom 3:21–26), and the Church’s approach becomes bold because it is mediated by him (Heb 10:19–22).

    2) The Church as participatory body: “living sacrifice” (Rom 12:1–2)

    Paul’s command in Rom 12:1–2 stands on a prior gospel logic: the mercies of God unveiled in Christ’s atoning work and Spirit‑given life (Rom 3–8). Because we have been united to Christ (Rom 6:1–11) and freed from condemnation (Rom 8:1–4), our bodies are no longer instruments of death but sites of worship. “Living sacrifice” is therefore not an attempt to add to Christ; it is the embodied consequence of being incorporated into him.

    This is also nous formation: “be transformed by the renewal of your mind” (Rom 12:2). The sacrificial life is not mere activism; it is a re‑ordered perception—desire retrained toward God by the Spirit‑shaped ordo of the Church (Acts 2:42). Paul’s logic is liturgical: worship forms the mind; the renewed mind offers the body.

    3) Eucharistic communion as sacrificial participation: the Table fulfills altar‑communion (1 Cor 10–11)

    Paul describes the cup and bread as “participation” (koinōnia) in Christ’s blood and body (1 Cor 10:16). This is not bare symbolism. It is communion that makes the Church one body: “because there is one bread, we who are many are one body” (1 Cor 10:17). The Table is therefore not an alternative to sacrifice but the sacrificial communion fulfilled: the peace‑offering logic (communion after atonement) is transfigured as Eucharistic communion in the once‑for‑all offering.

    Paul’s warning in 1 Cor 11 underscores the same realism: one can eat and drink “unworthily” and thus sin against the body and blood (1 Cor 11:27–29). The Eucharist is covenantal encounter. The Church does not repeat Christ’s sacrifice; she participates in it through anamnesis—a making present of God’s saving act—and through the Spirit’s epiclesis, who makes this communion effective in the Body‑Temple.

    4) Sacrificial living as kingdom witness: the Lamb enthrones a priestly people (Rev 5:6–10)

    Revelation ties sacrifice to kingship and priesthood. The Lamb is slain yet enthroned (Rev 5:6–10). His blood ransoms a people “and made them a kingdom and priests” (Rev 5:9–10). This is the same movement as Exodus 19 and 1 Peter 2: royal‑priestly identity emerges from sacrificial redemption (Exod 19:5–6; 1 Pet 2:9).

    Therefore the Church’s sacrificial life is not private piety; it is kingdom witness. Mercy, justice, and cruciform love are not adjuncts to worship; they are the shape of a people formed by participation in the Lamb.

    Patristic Turn: Sacrifice Fulfilled, Eucharist Made Present, Life Offered

    The Fathers read Leviticus through Christ, and Christ through the Church’s participation.

    1) Irenaeus speaks in the grammar of recapitulation: Christ gathers creation and humanity into himself, healing what was corrupted and offering it to the Father. The Eucharist, for Irenaeus, is not a human invention but the Church’s act of thanksgiving in which creation (bread and wine) is taken up into Christ’s redeeming work—thereby demanding a life congruent with that gift (cf. 1 Cor 10:16–17).¹

    2) Athanasius insists that the Word assumes flesh to renew humanity from within. If the body is renewed, worship cannot remain merely internal. The incarnation is already the beginning of sacrificial transformation: Christ offers his body unto death to abolish corruption and to make humanity again a dwelling fit for God (2 Cor 5:14–21; Eph 2:19–22).²

    3) Cyril of Jerusalem teaches Eucharistic participation in a way that presupposes epiclesis: the Spirit’s invocation mediates communion, transforming the earthly into participation in the heavenly. Cyril’s mystagogy is not speculative; it is ecclesial formation of the nous by sacramental encounter.³

    4) Chrysostom preaches both Hebrews and the Eucharist with the same insistence: the sacrifice is once‑for‑all, and yet the Church truly communes in it. His ethical force is severe and healing: one cannot honor Christ on the altar and despise him in the poor (Heb 13:16; Matt 25:40). Eucharistic communion generates merciful communion—or it becomes judgment (1 Cor 11:29).⁴

    5) Augustine offers the synthesis that guards against re‑sacrifice while preserving realism: the Church’s “true sacrifice” is the whole redeemed community offered in Christ the Head (City of God X). The Eucharist is the sacramental enactment of this identity: the Church receives what she is becoming, and she becomes what she receives (1 Cor 10:17).⁵

    6) Maximus the Confessor extends the logic cosmically: the liturgy gathers the world, and the sacrificial life is the Church’s participation in Christ’s offering that reorders creation through love. Sacrifice is not confined to the sanctuary; it becomes the form of existence for the Body‑Temple whose telos is covenantal love.⁶

    Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis): From Altar to Table, and From Table to Living Sacrifice

    We can now articulate the chapter’s constructive claim in three linked movements.

    1) The Levitical sacrifices are fulfilled in Christ’s once‑for‑all offering

    Hebrews’ argument is decisive: Leviticus is shadow; Christ is substance (Heb 10:1). The repeated offerings cannot cleanse conscience; Christ’s offering can (Heb 9:13–14). Therefore any Christian account of sacrifice begins with confession of sufficiency: “by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (Heb 10:14). The Church has no parallel altar competing with Christ.

    2) The Church participates in the one sacrifice through Eucharistic anamnesis and Spirit‑mediated communion

    Because anamnesis is a making‑present of God’s saving act, the Eucharist is not mere recollection (Luke 22:19; 1 Cor 11:26). Yet because the offering is once‑for‑all, Eucharistic presence cannot be repetition. The Church’s confession must therefore be participatory: the Table is where the Spirit makes the Church’s anamnesis effective communion in the one offering—epiclesis as God‑active mediation (1 Cor 10:16–17; Heb 10:19–22).

    This is precisely why 1 Corinthians binds Eucharist to ecclesial unity: participation in Christ’s body makes the Church one body (1 Cor 10:17). The Table is not only individual grace; it constitutes the Body‑Temple.

    3) Sacrifice is transfigured into the Church’s embodied life: living sacrifice as royal‑priestly witness

    From the Table, the Church is sent to live what she has received. “Present your bodies” is the ethical translation of Eucharistic communion into daily life (Rom 12:1–2). Hebrews likewise moves from altar to ethics: praise and sharing are sacrifices pleasing to God (Heb 13:15–16). In the Church, sacrifice becomes the shape of love: mercy enacted, enemies forgiven, goods shared, justice pursued, suffering borne with hope (Matt 25:31–46; Rom 12:9–21).

    This is not moralism. It is participation: the Spirit who incorporates us into Christ’s self‑offering forms a people whose life becomes a visible icon of the Lamb’s reign (Rev 5:9–10; 19:7–8). The telos is covenantal love—sacrificial, communal, and eschatological (1 Cor 13:8; Rev 19:8).

    Payoff/Implications (worship/ethics/formation)

    Worship: The Table as fulfilled sacrificial communion

    The Church approaches the Table not as a religious metaphor but as covenant renewal and real participation in Christ’s once‑for‑all offering (1 Cor 10:16; Heb 10:19–22). This guards reverence and joy: reverence because it is holy encounter (1 Cor 11:27–29), joy because it is peace‑offering communion fulfilled—God’s royal hospitality given in Christ.

    Ethics: Mercy as the outward form of sacrificial participation

    If Eucharist is communion in the self‑offering Lamb, then mercy is not optional. Doing good and sharing are named “sacrifices” (Heb 13:16). The Church’s care for “the least of these” is care for Christ (Matt 25:40). The sacrificial life manifests kingdom reality in public.

    Formation: The renewal of the nous by sacrificial logic

    Rom 12 joins living sacrifice to renewed mind (Rom 12:1–2). The ordo retrains desire: Word names reality; Table gives communion; Prayer sustains intercession. This forms the nous to see possessions, time, suffering, and neighbor through the Lamb’s pattern (Rev 5:6–10). Formation is therefore liturgical before it is managerial: the Church becomes what she worships.

    Objections & Replies (3–5 strongest)

    Objection 1: “If Christ fulfilled sacrifice, we should stop using sacrificial language.”

    Reply: The New Testament intensifies sacrificial language precisely after Christ’s fulfillment: living sacrifice (Rom 12:1), spiritual sacrifices (1 Pet 2:5), sacrifice of praise and sharing (Heb 13:15–16). Fulfillment does not remove the category; it transfigures it from repeated cult to participatory life in Christ.

    Objection 2: “If Eucharist is participation in Christ’s body and blood, that implies repetition of the sacrifice.”

    Reply: Hebrews explicitly rejects repetition (Heb 9:25–28; 10:10–14). Eucharistic anamnesis is a making‑present for participation, not a second offering. The Spirit’s epiclesis mediates communion in the one offering; it does not generate a parallel oblation (Luke 22:19; 1 Cor 11:26).

    Objection 3: “Calling ethics ‘sacrifice’ risks moralism and works‑righteousness.”

    Reply: Paul roots “living sacrifice” in “the mercies of God” (Rom 12:1) and in prior union with Christ (Rom 6:1–11; 8:1–4). Sacrificial living is fruit of participation, not wage. It is the embodied outworking of covenant communion, not a ladder to earn access (Heb 10:19–22).

    Objection 4: “Sacrificial theology is violent and should be abandoned.”

    Reply: Scripture locates the meaning of sacrifice not in human violence but in divine self‑giving love: the Son gives his life as ransom (Mark 10:45), and Christ “gave himself up for us” as fragrant offering (Eph 5:2). The Lamb’s enthronement is the enthronement of self‑offering mercy (Rev 5:6–10). Christian sacrifice is therefore the refusal of coercion and the embrace of cruciform love—especially for the vulnerable (Heb 13:16; Matt 25:31–46).

    Objection 5: “This makes the Church too central; doesn’t it distract from Christ?”

    Reply: The Church is central only as participant, not as source. Hebrews centers everything in Christ’s priesthood (Heb 9:24; 10:14). The Church’s priestly identity is derivative: a people made priests by the Lamb’s blood (Rev 5:9–10; 1 Pet 2:9). To speak of the Body‑Temple is to confess Christ’s ongoing mediation by the Spirit in the ecclesial assembly (Eph 2:18–22).
