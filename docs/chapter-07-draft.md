# Chapter 7 — Sacred Time & Anamnesis

**Thesis (≤2 sentences):**  
Biblical remembrance is not mere mental recall but anamnesis: the Church’s Spirit‑given “making present” of God’s saving acts so that a people is constituted in sacred time as firstfruits of the new creation (Exod 12:24–27; Luke 22:19–20; 1 Cor 11:23–26). Because Christ is the hermeneutical Key and the Spirit is God‑active, the Lord’s Day and the liturgical year become an eschatological pedagogy that trains the nous to inhabit “today” and to taste the powers of the age to come (Rev 1:10; Heb 12:22–24; Heb 6:4–5).

**Liturgical Integration:** [season/feast/hymn/lectionary]  
The Church’s weekly Lord’s Day assembly is the stable pulse of sacred time: the first day and the “eighth” day, the day of resurrection and new creation (Matt 28:1; John 20:19; Rev 1:10). The lectionary’s Paschal arc—Lent to Pascha to Pentecost—does not merely recollect; it initiates the Church into a shared “today,” culminating in the fifty days as an extended confession that the age to come has begun (Acts 2:1–4; Heb 6:4–5). Hymnody and psalmody repeatedly name the same ontological claim: “This is the day the LORD has made” (Ps 118:24), a doxology that trains the nous to receive time as gift rather than as enemy. In Eucharistic prayer, the Church performs anamnesis (“Do this in anamnesis of me,” Luke 22:19) and confesses that the Spirit is God‑active in making participation real (1 Cor 10:16–17), so that worship is already an approach to heavenly Zion (Heb 12:22–24).

**Practical Outcomes:** [pastoral/catechetical/ethical]  
1. **Pastoral:** Teach sufferers to locate their days within the Church’s “greater day”—Lord’s Day and seasons as a scaffold of hope when interior stamina fails (Ps 95:7–8; Heb 10:23–25).  
2. **Catechetical:** Define anamnesis explicitly (not mere recall) and relate it to Passover and the Eucharist so people grasp why the Church “does this” (Exod 12:24–27; 1 Cor 11:26).  
3. **Ethical:** Restore the prophetic ballast of sacred time: Sabbath and feasts are not escapism but a training in mercy and delight in God, resisting economic and technological tyranny (Isa 58:13–14; Mark 2:27).  
4. **Formation:** Encourage a simple “daily ordo” tethered to weekly worship—psalm, brief Scripture, thanksgiving—so the nous learns to inhabit time eucharistically (Rom 12:1–2; Heb 4:9–11).

## Chapter Body (drafted in the blueprint’s logic)

### Setup/Context: God forms a people by sacred time, not only sacred space
Part I has already named the foundational fabric: God speaks to form a people (Exod 19:5–6; Rom 10:17), and covenant is enacted liturgically as mediated presence (Exod 24:8–11; 25:22). Yet Scripture presses the pattern further. The Covenant–Temple drama is never confined to architecture. God also gathers His people by time—by days, weeks, seasons—so that remembrance becomes a communal, embodied participation.

Israel learns this at Passover. The Lord does not merely announce deliverance; He commands a rite that must be kept “as a memorial day” and “a feast… throughout your generations” (Exod 12:14). The point is not nostalgia but identity: “When your children say… you shall say, ‘It is the sacrifice of the LORD’s Passover’” (Exod 12:26–27). Sacred time becomes the pedagogy of a people. God’s saving act is not left behind as a datum in the past; it is carried forward as an enacted “today.”

The Church inherits this grammar, now fulfilled. The resurrection occurs “on the first day of the week” (Matt 28:1; John 20:1), and the risen Christ repeatedly appears to the gathered disciples in that rhythm (John 20:19, 26). The earliest Christians therefore assemble “on the first day of the week” to break bread (Acts 20:7), and John names this ecclesial time as “the Lord’s Day” (Rev 1:10). Sacred time becomes the Church’s stable pulse of participation: not a preference for religious scheduling, but the covenantal form by which the risen Christ gathers His body for Word and Table (1 Cor 10:16–17).

### Question‑at‑Issue: How can the past be present without repetition—and why does the Church need ordered time?
The doctrinal puzzle is sharp. If Christ’s offering is once‑for‑all (Heb 10:10–14), how can the Church truthfully obey, “Do this in anamnesis of me,” without implying a repeated sacrifice or a rival cult (Luke 22:19–20)? And if the gospel is eternal, why does Scripture insist that God forms a people through days and seasons—Passover, Sabbath, the Lord’s Day—rather than through a “timeless” interior spirituality (Exod 12:14; Heb 4:9–11)?

The question matters because modern instincts pull us toward two errors. Either we flatten remembrance into cognition—“we think about what happened”—or we suspect sacred time of being merely symbolic—“church calendar customs.” But Scripture’s language will not cooperate. Passover is commanded as a memorial that shapes identity (Exod 12:24–27), and the Eucharist is commanded as an action that proclaims and participates in the Lord’s death “until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26). Hebrews, meanwhile, describes worship as approach to heavenly Zion—not as aspiration but as present ecclesial reality (Heb 12:22–24). We must therefore recover a participatory account: sacred time as covenantal, Spirit‑mediated anamnesis.

### Prior Answers (why insufficient): three reductions of sacred time
1. **Remembrance as mere cognition.** Here “anamnesis” becomes mental recollection. Yet Passover is not a lecture about Exodus; it is a commanded enactment by which the community becomes “the delivered people” again and again (Exod 13:3–10). Likewise, Paul does not say, “Think about the cross,” but “Do this… you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:24–26). The act is ecclesial and formative.

2. **Sacred time as optional symbolism.** Here feast and Lord’s Day are treated as religious aesthetics without theological necessity. But Scripture frames time as covenantal sign: Sabbath is given “that you may know that I, the LORD, sanctify you” (Ezek 20:12, 20). The Lord’s Day is similarly a sign of new creation: the resurrection sets a new temporal axis for the people gathered in Christ (John 20:19; Rev 1:10).

3. **Liturgy as repetition competing with the once‑for‑all.** Here the Eucharist is either rejected as dangerous repetition or emptied into mere memorialism to avoid that danger. But the New Testament holds together once‑for‑all accomplishment and present participation: the Church has “confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus” and therefore must not neglect assembly (Heb 10:19–25). The solution is not denial of presence but a right account of mediation: anamnesis is the Spirit’s making‑present of the one saving act for participation, not its re‑performance.

These reductions fail because they treat time as neutral and worship as expressive rather than constitutive. Scripture reveals that God converts time into covenantal form so that the Church may live between the ages.

### Scripture/Event Anchor: Passover and Eucharist—enacted memory that constitutes a people
The decisive anchor is the paired structure of Exodus 12 and Luke 22/1 Corinthians 11.

In Exodus, the Lord binds deliverance to a commanded rite. The lamb’s blood marks the people; the meal is eaten in haste; and the day is established as a perpetual memorial (Exod 12:7–14). The point is not merely to remember that deliverance occurred. The rite carries the event forward as communal identity. Israel becomes the people who live by an enacted memory: “It is because of what the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt” (Exod 13:8). Sacred time makes the saving act contemporaneous—not by collapsing chronology, but by covenantal participation.

Christ fulfills and transfigures this pattern at the Last Supper. He takes bread and cup and interprets His death as covenant: “This cup… is the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:20). Then He commands a practice: “Do this in anamnesis of me” (Luke 22:19). Paul receives and transmits the same tradition and adds its eschatological horizon: “as often as you eat… you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26). The act is oriented to past (death), present (proclamation/participation), and future (coming). Sacred time is therefore eschatological time: the Church lives in a “today” stretched between cross and parousia.

Hebrews supplies the ontological depth. Worship is not merely a gathering on earth with thoughts about heaven; it is an approach to “Mount Zion… the heavenly Jerusalem” (Heb 12:22–24). Sacred time, then, is not a psychological technique. It is the covenantal mode by which the Church is located where Christ is—by the Spirit—so that the people taste the coming city while still walking in the old world.

### Patristic Turn: the Church learns to inhabit time as participation
The Fathers receive this biblical grammar and speak of sacred time with both realism and reverence.

**Melito of Sardis** proclaims Pascha as fulfilled in Christ and preached as present reality. His homiletic thunder is not antiquarian: the saving act is announced as something that claims the hearers now—an ecclesial “today” in which deliverance is proclaimed and participated.

**Justin Martyr** offers historical witness to the Church’s weekly sacred time. The Church gathers on “the day called Sunday” because it is both the first day of creation and the day of resurrection; Word and Eucharist constitute the community (cf. Acts 20:7). Justin’s point is not merely schedule; it is ontology: the Church is gathered into a new creation rhythm.

**Irenaeus** binds Eucharist to new creation. If bread and wine—creaturely realities—become communion in Christ, then the Church receives a pledge of resurrection: the material world is being taken into the saving act (Rom 6:3–5). Sacred time is therefore not spiritual escape but the Church’s training to live as firstfruits.

**Cyril of Jerusalem** provides mystagogy: he teaches the baptized that participation is real and that the mysteries locate the Church within the saving act. His catecheses assume that anamnesis is not mere recollection but Spirit‑given nearness, forming a people for holiness and praise (1 Pet 2:5, 9).

**Augustine** guards the unity of worship and ethics by insisting that sacrament forms identity: the Church is made into what she receives—“Be what you see.” This makes sacred time morally weighty. The Lord’s Day and Eucharist do not merely comfort; they convert. They train the Church to become the totus Christus in sacrificial love (Rom 12:1–2).

**Maximus the Confessor** offers the deepest synthesis. Liturgy, for Maximus, is entry into the eschatological reality that the Church already tastes. Sacred time is therefore a cosmic pedagogy: the Church’s worship orders the nous, gathers the scattered, and teaches creation’s true telos in communion with God (Heb 12:22–24; Rev 5:9–14). Time becomes a liturgical instrument of deification—not by erasing time, but by transfiguring it.

The Fathers thus converge on a single claim: sacred time is the Church’s participation in God’s saving act and in God’s coming kingdom, mediated by the Spirit and centered in Christ.

### Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis): Sacred time as covenantal‑eschatological participation
We can now state the constructive synthesis with precision, using the series’ locked terms.

1. **Anamnesis is “making present,” not mere recall.** Passover is commanded as enacted memorial that constitutes identity (Exod 12:14; 12:26–27). The Eucharist is commanded as enacted remembrance that proclaims and participates in Christ’s once‑for‑all death “until he comes” (Luke 22:19–20; 1 Cor 11:26). Anamnesis therefore belongs to the Covenant–Temple pattern: it is covenant renewal through liturgical enactment.

2. **The Spirit is God‑active in sacred time.** Sacred time is not powered by human imagination. Participation is Spirit‑mediated: communion is koinōnia in Christ (1 Cor 10:16–17), and worship is approach to heavenly Zion (Heb 12:22–24). The epiclesis (explicit or implicit) names what Scripture reveals: the Spirit transforms the earthly into participation in the heavenly.

3. **Sacred time is eschatological time.** The Church lives between the ages: she already tastes “the powers of the age to come” (Heb 6:4–5) even as she awaits the appearing of Christ (1 Cor 11:26). The Lord’s Day is therefore not merely weekly rest but weekly resurrection: the Church is trained to live from the future.

4. **Sacred time forms the nous toward the telos of covenantal love.** Time is not neutral. The Church’s ordo and calendar train attention, desire, and action. Sabbath and Lord’s Day resist the tyranny of endless production and cultivate delight in God that issues in mercy (Isa 58:13–14; Mark 2:27). Sacred time becomes an ethics of availability: we learn to live as a priestly people whose days are offered.

Thus sacred time is not decorative. It is the covenantal‑eschatological medium by which God gathers the Church into participation, making her a people who already taste the coming city.

### Payoff/Implications (worship/ethics/formation): learning “today” as the Church’s posture
**Worship:** The Lord’s Day is the Church’s weekly Pascha. The assembly does not merely meet to discuss redemption; it is gathered into the risen Christ’s presence through Word and Table (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor 10:16–17). Sacred time therefore protects worship from becoming event‑culture: it is covenantal rhythm, stable and formative.

**Ethics:** Sacred time contains prophetic ballast. Sabbath is given so that Israel remembers deliverance and therefore refuses Pharaoh’s logic of endless labor (Deut 5:12–15). The Church, living from resurrection, resists new Pharaohs—economic, technological, and ideological—by receiving time as gift for mercy and worship (Mark 2:27; Isa 58:6–12). The day of the Lord trains a people whose work is ordered toward love.

**Formation:** Sacred time trains the nous to inhabit “today.” Psalm 95’s liturgical warning—“Today, if you hear his voice”—is not a private slogan but an ecclesial discipline (Ps 95:7–8; Heb 3:7–15). Weekly worship, seasons, and daily prayer form a stable attentiveness: the Church learns to recognize time as the arena where covenantal presence is received and enacted. In this way, the people who remember become the people who persevere, already tasting what they will one day see (Heb 10:23–25; 21:3–5).

Part I thus completes another foundational reality. God forms a people by speech, by presence, and by sacred time. The next chapter will name the internal “rhythm” by which these realities converge in weekly worship: the ordo of Word, Table, and Prayer.

**Objections & Replies:** [3–5 strongest]

1. **Objection: “Does anamnesis imply a repeated sacrifice, contradicting Hebrews?”**  
   **Reply:** Hebrews denies repetition while commanding present access and assembly (Heb 10:10–14, 19–25). The Eucharist is participation in the one offering, not a parallel cult: “as often as you eat… you proclaim… until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26). Anamnesis is the Spirit‑given making‑present of the once‑for‑all for communion, not re‑crucifixion (Luke 22:19).

2. **Objection: “Isn’t sacred time just ‘Jewish’ scaffolding that Christians outgrow?”**  
   **Reply:** Christ fulfills rather than abolishes. The Lord’s Day arises from resurrection and is named in Scripture (John 20:19; Rev 1:10), and the Church is commanded to gather (Heb 10:24–25). Colossians warns against condemnation over shadows (Col 2:16–17), not against the Church’s Spirit‑formed rhythm of participation in the substance, which is Christ.

3. **Objection: “Does liturgical time make spirituality mechanical?”**  
   **Reply:** Scripture’s remedy for mechanical worship is not to abandon form but to restore covenant truth and mercy (Isa 58:6–12). Sacred time is pedagogical: it trains the nous and frees the Church from being ruled by mood. The Spirit is God‑active, not a technique; the rhythm is a means of communion, not an engine of control (2 Cor 3:16–18).

4. **Objection: “If remembrance is participation, is it subjective—dependent on intensity of experience?”**  
   **Reply:** No. Sacred time is ecclesial and objective in its instituted signs: Passover is commanded enactment (Exod 12:14), and the Eucharist is commanded practice (Luke 22:19). Experience may vary, but the covenantal act remains God’s gift to His people, received by faith within the ordo (Heb 12:22–24).

5. **Objection: “Why insist on the Lord’s Day if every day belongs to God?”**  
   **Reply:** Every day belongs to God, yet Scripture repeatedly sets apart times as covenantal signs (Ezek 20:12; Lev 23). The Lord’s Day is not a denial of daily sanctity but its source and pattern: weekly resurrection worship re‑orients the whole week toward the telos of covenantal love (Heb 4:9–11; Rom 12:1–2).
