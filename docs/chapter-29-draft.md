# Chapter 29: Households as Little Covenant–Temples (Deut 6; Eph 5–6)

**Thesis (≤2 sentences):**  
The household is a micro‑temple where **covenant** is rehearsed in time, speech, meals, work, and mercy: the Church’s **ordo** (Word · Table · Prayer) is extended into domestic space so that *anamnesis* becomes daily remembrance‑participation and the **telos** of life is sacrificial, covenantal love (Deut 6:4–9; Acts 2:42; Eph 5:21–6:4). Under Christ’s headship, the Spirit constitutes households as sites of formation where authority is re‑patterned as mutual self‑offering, and ordinary tasks become priestly service (Eph 5:21–33; Col 3:17; Rom 12:1).

**Biblical Ground (passages):**  
Deut 6:4–9; Josh 24:15; Ps 127:1; Acts 2:42, 46–47; Rom 12:1–2; Eph 5:18–6:4; Col 3:12–17; 1 Tim 3:4–5; Heb 13:15–16; 1 Pet 2:5, 9.

**Argument Map (Wilken‑calibrated):**  
[Setup/Context → Question‑at‑Issue → Prior Answers (insufficient) → Scripture/Event Anchor → Patristic Turn → Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis) → Payoff/Implications]

### One‑liners for each move (keep it tight)
* **Setup/Context:** The Church’s Sunday worship trains the *nous* through Word, Table, and Prayer, yet without a domestic extension the week becomes a counter‑liturgy shaped by anxiety and habit (Acts 2:42, 46; Rom 12:2).  
* **Question‑at‑Issue:** How does the Church’s liturgical–covenantal participation become a stable domestic form—so that households are not merely “private life,” but an ecclesial arena of formation and mercy (Deut 6:4–9; Eph 5:21)?  
* **Prior Answers (insufficient):** (1) **Privatized piety** confines faith to interior sentiment, leaving household speech and economy unconverted (Col 3:16–17); (2) **Bare moralism** reduces family life to rule‑keeping without sacramental participation (Rom 12:1–2); (3) **Authority as domination** contradicts the cruciform pattern of Christ’s headship (Eph 5:25).  
* **Scripture/Event Anchor:** Deut 6 locates covenant fidelity in the household’s daily rhythms—words on the heart, speech “when you sit… walk… lie down… rise” (Deut 6:6–7); Eph 5–6 reconstitutes household relations “in the Lord,” beginning with mutual submission and Spirit‑filled thanksgiving (Eph 5:18–21; 6:1–4).  
* **Patristic Turn:** Chrysostom names the home a “little church,” where Scripture, prayer, and almsgiving enact domestic liturgy; Augustine frames domestic discipline as ordered love (*ordo amoris*) oriented to God (Col 3:16; Heb 13:16).  
* **Doctrinal Resolution:** The household becomes a little covenant–temple when it is ordered by the Church’s **ordo**: Word (teaching and psalmody), Table (thanksgiving and shared meals as *anamnesis*), Prayer (intercession and reconciliation), all enacted by the Spirit toward the **telos** of sacrificial, covenantal love (Deut 6:4–9; Acts 2:46; Eph 5:18–21).  
* **Payoff/Implications:** Domestic space becomes a site of ecclesial formation: children are catechized, spouses and parents learn cruciform authority, work and money are consecrated, and mercy becomes habitual “liturgy after the liturgy” (Eph 6:4; Heb 13:15–16).

## 1. Deuteronomy’s Domestic Covenant: The First Pedagogy of the Household (Deut 6:4–9)

Israel’s covenant pedagogy is deliberately domestic. The Shema (“Hear, O Israel…”) begins not with technique but with a liturgical summons to whole‑person allegiance: heart, soul, and might (Deut 6:4–5). Immediately, Scripture relocates that allegiance into the texture of daily habitation: the words are “on your heart,” spoken and repeated within ordinary time—sitting, walking, lying down, rising (Deut 6:6–7). The covenant is therefore not maintained by episodic religious intensity but by a patterned rhythm of remembrance, speech, and embodied practice.

This is already temple logic. The sanctuary is the concentrated site where the people learn who God is; the household is the distributed site where that confession becomes durable. The same movement appears later: Joshua’s household vow (“as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD”) is a covenantal public claim with domestic scope (Josh 24:15). And the Psalter refuses any illusion of self‑construction: “unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain” (Ps 127:1). The household is a place God must build—precisely because it is a formative architecture shaping desire and memory.

For the Church, Deut 6 cannot be treated as merely “Old Testament parenting advice.” It is the prototype of how covenant is transmitted: by a liturgical–covenantal rhythm that forms the *nous* and trains love. Thus the Church reads Deut 6 in continuity with her own rule: “devoted to the apostles’ teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers” (Acts 2:42). When Luke notes that the Church broke bread “at home” with gladness and simplicity of heart (Acts 2:46), domestic space becomes an extension of ecclesial worship rather than a competitor to it.

**SBL‑style note:** Deut 6:7 (“repeat them diligently…”) binds catechesis to time and place, making household habit a primary medium of covenant formation rather than a secondary add‑on.

## 2. The Household in Christ: Spirit‑Filled Order as Ecclesial Participation (Eph 5:18–21)

Ephesians does not offer a free‑standing social ethic; it describes a Spirit‑constituted community whose life is Eucharistic in texture. The movement is explicit: “be filled with the Spirit,” then singing, thanksgiving, and mutual submission “in the fear of Christ” (Eph 5:18–21). Domestic relationships follow as an extension of this Spirit‑filled worship, not as a retreat into private governance (Eph 5:22–6:4).

Two claims follow.

First, the household is not merely natural; it is ecclesial in orientation. Paul’s “in the Lord” language is not ornamental: children obey “in the Lord” (Eph 6:1), fathers discipline “of the Lord” (Eph 6:4), and marital love is patterned on Christ and the Church (Eph 5:25–32). The domestic sphere is therefore drawn into the covenant–temple drama: Christ’s self‑offering becomes the pattern of authority, and the Spirit’s filling becomes the power of endurance and peace (Eph 5:18; 5:25).

Second, the household is ordered toward **telos**. Paul frames the entire section by love and holiness: Christ gives himself “to sanctify” and present the Church glorious (Eph 5:25–27). Domestic life is one of the primary arenas where that sanctification is enacted because it is where speech habits, anger patterns, forgiveness, and gentleness are tested daily (cf. Eph 4:31–32; Col 3:12–15). What is practiced there is not morally neutral: it forms either a covenantal people or a fragmented set of individuals.

**SBL‑style note:** Eph 5:18–21 provides the liturgical matrix for Eph 5:22–6:4; domestic ethics is downstream from Spirit‑filled praise and thanksgiving.

## 3. Marital Love as Micro‑Liturgy: Headship Refigured as Self‑Offering (Eph 5:21–33)

Ephesians begins the household code not with hierarchy but with reciprocity: “submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ” (Eph 5:21). This does not erase asymmetries of role language that follow; it refigures their meaning by the pattern of Christ. The decisive interpretive key is v. 25: husbands love “as Christ loved the Church and gave himself up for her” (Eph 5:25). Authority is therefore cruciform; it is measured by self‑offering, not by domination.

Here the book’s locked terms help us name the theological density. The household becomes a “little covenant–temple” when its relational order participates in the one sacrifice of Christ rather than mimicking the world’s rival liturgies of control, resentment, or self‑protection (Rom 12:2; Eph 5:25). Domestic love is therefore not sentimental. It is priestly: it offers the self so that the other may flourish in holiness, truth, and peace (Eph 5:26–27; Col 3:13–14).

The Church should also notice that Paul’s marital language is sacramental in logic: cleansing/washing imagery echoes baptismal participation (Eph 5:26; cf. Titus 3:5), and the union language (“one flesh”) is placed within the mystery of Christ and the Church (Eph 5:31–32). Domestic union is not the source of the mystery; it is a participated icon of it.

**SBL‑style note:** Eph 5:31–32 treats “one flesh” as a sign participating in a greater mystery—Christ and the Church—so domestic love is inherently ecclesial in reference.

## 4. Parenting as Priestly Formation: The Household as Catechetical Temple (Deut 6; Eph 6:1–4)

Children are not an interruption to “spiritual life”; they are a primary locus of covenant transmission. Deut 6 commands the household to speak God’s words across the whole day (Deut 6:6–7). Paul intensifies this in Christ: children obey “in the Lord” (Eph 6:1), and parents are commanded to avoid provoking resentment while bringing children up in the “discipline and instruction of the Lord” (Eph 6:4). The household is therefore not merely a protective unit; it is a catechetical sanctuary.

This is where the Church’s **ordo** becomes domestic pedagogy:

* **Word:** Scripture and psalmody must become audible in the household (Deut 6:7; Col 3:16).  
* **Table:** shared meals become thanksgiving rather than consumption alone, training gratitude as a stable disposition (Eph 5:20; Acts 2:46).  
* **Prayer:** intercession names persons and needs, and reconciliation is practiced as a domestic rite of peace (Matt 5:23–24; Eph 4:32).

The result is not perfection but stability: a home that teaches repentance and forgiveness as normal, and mercy as expected. Such domestic formation directly affects ecclesial life; Paul connects household governance to Church oversight (1 Tim 3:4–5), not because family success is a credential, but because the household reveals whether a person has learned cruciform care.

**SBL‑style note:** Eph 6:4 (“do not provoke… but bring them up…”) places parental power under a restraint that mirrors Christ’s gentleness (Matt 11:29) and the Spirit’s fruit (Gal 5:22–23).

## 5. Domestic Economy and Mercy: Households as Sites of “Liturgy After the Liturgy” (Heb 13:15–16)

A temple is measured not only by words but by offerings. Hebrews gives a succinct New Covenant “sacrificial” grammar: offer praise, and do good and share, “for with such sacrifices God is pleased” (Heb 13:15–16). If the Church’s Eucharistic *anamnesis* makes present the self‑offering of Christ, then domestic life must become an echo of that offering in money, hospitality, and attentiveness (Rom 12:1; Heb 13:16). The home becomes a little covenant–temple when it is porous to the neighbor and the poor rather than sealed as a private fortress (Matt 25:35; Heb 13:2).

Acts again supplies a picture: the earliest Church received food with gladness and generosity, and the Lord added to their number (Acts 2:46–47). Domestic meals became evangelically luminous because they were not merely functional; they were Eucharistic in spirit—thanksgiving shaping an open table. This does not require constant hosting; it requires a household economy that is not ruled by scarcity or status (Luke 12:15; 1 Tim 6:17–19). The domestic temple offers not animals but the self, money, and time as sacrificial mercy (Heb 13:16).

**SBL‑style note:** Heb 13:16 calls doing good and sharing “sacrifices,” explicitly relocating temple language into ethical action—precisely the movement this Part IX presses.

**Objections & Replies:** [3–5 strongest]

1) **Objection:** “Calling the household a ‘little covenant–temple’ spiritualizes family life and distracts from the Church.”  
   **Reply:** Scripture moves in the opposite direction: it extends ecclesial participation into domestic time (Acts 2:42, 46). The household is not a rival sanctuary but a satellite arena where the Church’s **ordo** is enacted. Domestic formation is therefore ecclesial obedience, not replacement.

2) **Objection:** “Deut 6 is Israel’s covenant; applying it to Christian households risks flattening redemptive history.”  
   **Reply:** The Church reads Deut 6 through Christ and the Spirit: the same God forms a people by word and rhythm, now “in the Lord” (Eph 6:1) and in Spirit‑filled thanksgiving (Eph 5:18–21). Continuity is covenantal pedagogy fulfilled, not simple repetition (Rom 11:17–24).

3) **Objection:** “Eph 5–6 can justify oppressive authority structures.”  
   **Reply:** Paul’s controlling interpretive claim is cruciform: Christ’s headship is defined by self‑giving love (Eph 5:25) and the whole section is framed by mutual submission (Eph 5:21). Any domination contradicts the text’s Christological pattern and the Spirit’s fruit (Gal 5:22–23).

4) **Objection:** “Domestic ‘ordo’ sounds like a burdensome program; families are already exhausted.”  
   **Reply:** The ordo is minimal and participatory, not maximal and performative: it begins with the Spirit’s filling (Eph 5:18) and aims at stability rather than intensity (Deut 6:7). Scripture’s pattern is small, repeatable acts—words on the heart, daily speech, thanksgiving—precisely because the covenant is sustained by mercy, not strain (Rom 12:1).

5) **Objection:** “Hospitality and almsgiving are optional charisms, not household obligations.”  
   **Reply:** Hebrews calls doing good and sharing “sacrifices pleasing to God” (Heb 13:16). Acts describes domestic meals and generosity as normal Christian life (Acts 2:46–47). The forms vary by capacity, but the covenantal direction—toward mercy—is not optional because the **telos** is sacrificial, covenantal love.
