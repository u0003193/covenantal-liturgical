    Chapter 31: Suffering and the Church’s Carrying Prayer (Ps 22; 2 Cor 1; Rom 8)

    Thesis (≤2 sentences):
    Suffering is not merely a private ordeal but an ecclesial arena in which the Spirit constitutes the Church as a praying Body that carries one another into Christ’s own lament and intercession (Ps 22:1–5; Rom 8:26–27). In the daily ordo—Offer → Open nous → Discern/Love → Do—the Church learns to practice anamnesis of God’s faithfulness so that affliction becomes a place of participation, consolation, and covenantal love rather than isolation (2 Cor 1:3–7; Rom 12:1–2).

    Biblical Ground (passages):
    Ps 22; 2 Cor 1:3–11; Rom 8:18–39 (esp. 8:26–39); Heb 4:14–16; Heb 5:7–9; Lam 3:19–33; Phil 3:8–11; Col 1:24; Jas 5:13–16; Acts 12:5; Mark 14:32–42; John 11:33–44; 1 Pet 4:12–19.

    Argument Map (Wilken‑calibrated):
    [Setup/Context → Question‑at‑Issue → Prior Answers (insufficient) → Scripture/Event Anchor → Patristic Turn → Doctrinal Resolution (Synthesis) → Payoff/Implications]

    One‑liners for each move (keep it tight)

    • Setup/Context: The Church’s daily life is not only vocation and economy but also affliction—illness, betrayal, grief, persecution—and these pressures tempt the sufferer into solitude and the community into silence (2 Cor 1:8–9; Ps 22:1; Jas 5:14).
    • Question‑at‑Issue: How can suffering be inhabited as participation in Christ and not as covenantal abandonment—so that lament becomes prayer and prayer becomes shared carrying (Ps 22:1–5; Rom 8:26–27)?
    • Prior Answers (insufficient): (1) Stoic containment that treats lament as weakness (Ps 22:2); (2) Therapeutic privatization that reduces suffering to interior management without ecclesial mediation (Jas 5:16); (3) Punitive theodicy that treats affliction as simple retribution and thus mutes consolation (John 9:2–3; Lam 3:31–33).
    • Scripture/Event Anchor: Psalm 22 gives the canonical grammar of lament that holds accusation and trust together; Paul names the God of all comfort who comforts us so we can comfort others (2 Cor 1:3–4); Romans 8 declares the Spirit’s intercession in our weakness and the inseparability of the Church from the love of God in Christ (Rom 8:26–39).
    • Patristic Turn: Athanasius reads the Psalms as the Church’s voice in Christ; Augustine interprets Ps 22 as totus Christus—Head and Body praying; Chrysostom and Basil portray intercession as the Church’s priestly labor that bears the afflicted into divine consolation (Ps 22:1; Rom 8:26).
    • Doctrinal Resolution: The Church confesses suffering as a site of Spirit‑mediated participation: by epiclesis the Spirit joins our inarticulate groaning to Christ’s own prayer, opening the nous to discern covenantal presence even in darkness, so that consolation becomes ecclesial and affliction becomes a school of covenantal love (Rom 8:26–28; 2 Cor 1:5–7).
    • Payoff/Implications: Lament becomes liturgical rather than lonely; pastoral care becomes priestly mediation; and the daily ordo trains a community whose carrying prayer anticipates the eschatological telos where God wipes away every tear (Rom 8:18–25; Rev 21:4).

    The Scriptural Logic of Carrying Prayer: Lament → Consolation → Intercession

    Suffering raises a primary spiritual temptation: to interpret pain as abandonment. Scripture answers not by denying the darkness but by giving the Church a grammar to speak within it. Psalm 22 begins with the cry of dereliction—“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Ps 22:1)—yet it refuses to let the cry become a metaphysic. The psalm holds complaint (“I cry… but you do not answer,” Ps 22:2) together with remembered covenantal fidelity (“Yet you are holy… In you our fathers trusted,” Ps 22:3–4). This is not emotional oscillation; it is anamnesis—a making‑present of God’s past saving acts so that the sufferer may participate in the Church’s memory now (Ps 22:4–5). In this way, the psalm enacts the daily movement from affliction to worship: lament that remains prayer becomes a path into God rather than a flight from him (Ps 22:19–24).

    Paul’s opening in 2 Corinthians 1 places this psalmic grammar inside the Church’s communal life. God is “the Father of mercies and God of all comfort” (2 Cor 1:3), and the comfort he gives is not terminal but transmissive: “who comforts us… so that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction” (2 Cor 1:4). Comfort is thus ecclesial mediation. It is the Spirit’s work of constituting a community whose suffering becomes a shared burden and whose prayer becomes a shared carrying (2 Cor 1:6–7; cf. Gal 6:2). Paul even defines apostolic existence as a kind of paschal pattern: “we were so utterly burdened… we despaired of life itself” (2 Cor 1:8), “but that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead” (2 Cor 1:9). Affliction, then, becomes a participation in resurrection‑reliance—a deepening into the covenantal God who acts beyond human resource.

    Romans 8 brings the synthesis to its summit. Paul does not spiritualize pain; he places it inside creation’s groaning (Rom 8:22–23) and inside the Church’s adoption‑hope (Rom 8:23–25). Most crucially, he declares that the Spirit intercedes “in our weakness” when we do not know what to pray (Rom 8:26). The Spirit’s intercession is not a replacement of prayer but a divine carrying of prayer, a joining of our voiceless groans to the Son’s own intercession (Rom 8:26–27; cf. Heb 7:25). Here the Church receives a fundamental confession: suffering does not silence prayer; it reveals the deepest mode of prayer—petition borne by God’s own life.

    SBL‑style note: Rom 8:26–27 grounds intercession in the Spirit’s agency (ὑπερεντυγχάνει), locating prayer in Trinitarian mediation rather than in human capacity.

    Psalm 22 as Ecclesial Pedagogy: The Church Learns to Speak Without Lying (Ps 22; Heb 5:7)

    Psalm 22 is not a private diary; it is the Church’s school of truthful speech before God. It grants permission to speak the darkness while remaining within covenant address: “My God” still names God as God even when the heart cannot perceive him (Ps 22:1). That address is already a refusal of nihilism. The psalm then turns memory into resistance: the fathers trusted and were delivered (Ps 22:4–5). Memory here is not nostalgic; it is sacramental in structure—anamnesis as participation in the Church’s saving history.

    The New Testament intensifies this pedagogy by placing Psalm 22 on Christ’s lips at the cross (Matt 27:46; Mark 15:34). The cry does not mean the Father has ceased to love the Son; it means the Son has entered the deepest human experience of abandonment and has prayed from within it. The Church is therefore authorized to lament because Christ has lamented; lament is not faithlessness but participation in the crucified prayer of the Son.

    Hebrews strengthens this claim by portraying Jesus’ earthly life as priestly supplication: “In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears” (Heb 5:7). The Church discovers that tears are not outside priesthood; they belong to it. Christ’s “loud cries” (Heb 5:7) interpret psalmic lament as priestly action, a form of offering that passes through suffering into obedience and life (Heb 5:8–9). The suffering Church therefore does not invent a new liturgy; she is drawn into Christ’s own liturgical action—his intercession.

    SBL‑style note: Heb 5:7–9 frames Christ’s suffering as priestly prayer leading to perfected obedience, supporting the claim that lament can be a mode of faithful participation rather than unbelief.

    2 Corinthians 1: Consolation as Ecclesial Circulation (2 Cor 1:3–7)

    Paul’s logic is covenantal and communal. Comfort comes from “the Father of mercies” (2 Cor 1:3), which means consolation is not mere soothing; it is mercy enacted as presence. The key connective is “so that” (2 Cor 1:4): comfort is given so that it may become a shared capacity. The Church becomes a “circulation” of consolation, a living network of carrying prayer. Therefore, isolation is not only a psychological risk; it is an ecclesial fracture. To suffer alone is to be deprived of the Church’s mediating vocation (Jas 5:14–16; Acts 12:5).

    Paul also clarifies that shared suffering yields shared comfort: “as we share abundantly in Christ’s sufferings, so through Christ we share abundantly in comfort too” (2 Cor 1:5). The phrase “in Christ” is decisive: suffering is not romanticized, but it is located within participation. The Church does not seek affliction; she receives it when it comes and refuses to let it become meaningless. Instead, she prays it into Christ, who transfigures even affliction into a site where covenantal love is learned and enacted (2 Cor 1:6–7).

    SBL‑style note: 2 Cor 1:5–7 is explicitly participatory (“share… in Christ”), which anchors ecclesial consolation in union with Christ rather than in technique.

    Romans 8: The Spirit’s Groaning and the Inseparability of Love (Rom 8:18–39)

    Romans 8 joins three groanings: creation’s (Rom 8:22), the Church’s (Rom 8:23), and the Spirit’s (Rom 8:26). This triad places the Church’s suffering inside an eschatological horizon: the “revealing of the sons of God” (Rom 8:19) and the redemption of our bodies (Rom 8:23). Hope is not denial; it is a new kind of perception that the Spirit grants—a renewed nous able to discern that present sufferings are not the measure of final reality (Rom 8:18; Rom 12:2).

    The Spirit’s intercession is the heart of “carrying prayer.” When we “do not know what to pray for as we ought” (Rom 8:26), the Spirit bears our incapacity and turns it into petition. This means that weakness is not a disqualification from prayer; it becomes the place where divine mediation is most explicit. The Spirit “intercedes for the saints according to the will of God” (Rom 8:27), aligning our prayer with God’s purposes even when our speech collapses.

    Romans 8 then concludes with a litany of inseparability: tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, sword (Rom 8:35). None can separate the Church from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Rom 8:39). This is not sentimental assurance; it is covenantal permanence—God’s pledged fidelity enacted through Christ and sealed in the Spirit. The Church’s carrying prayer is therefore not merely empathy; it is participation in this inseparable love, enacted as intercession for one another (Rom 8:34, 26–27).

    SBL‑style note: Rom 8:35–39 frames affliction within covenantal love that cannot be severed, making carrying prayer an enactment of this inseparability rather than a strategy for coping.

    Patristic Witness (top 6 citations)

    1. Athanasius. Letter to Marcellinus on the Interpretation of the Psalms. (Psalms as Christ’s and the Church’s prayer; the Psalter forms affections and speech.)
    2. Augustine. Expositions of the Psalms (Enarrationes in Psalmos) on Ps 21[22]. (Totus Christus: Head and Body praying; the Church speaks in Christ.)
    3. John Chrysostom. Homilies on 2 Corinthians (on 2 Cor 1:3–7; comfort as ecclesial strengthening; suffering as shared participation).
    4. Basil of Caesarea. On the Holy Spirit and selected homilies/letters on prayer (Spirit’s agency in prayer; intercession as ecclesial work).
    5. Gregory of Nazianzus. Orations (especially theological orations and consolatory material; divine condescension and pastoral consolation).
    6. Cyprian of Carthage. On the Lord’s Prayer (prayer as ecclesial—“our” not “my”; the Church intercedes as one body).

    Annotated note (why these six): together they provide (a) psalmic formation (Athanasius), (b) christological-ecclesial reading of lament (Augustine), (c) apostolic pastoral realism (Chrysostom), (d) pneumatological grounding for intercession (epiclesis-shaped prayer) (Basil), (e) doctrinal and pastoral articulation of consolation (Gregory), and (f) ecclesial grammar of shared address (Cyprian).

    Liturgical Integration: [season/feast/hymn/lectionary]

    • Holy Week (Ps 22; Heb 5:7): The Church learns that lament is not outside the Passion but within it; the cry of dereliction becomes a canonical mode of participation in Christ’s priestly prayer (Ps 22:1; Heb 5:7).
    • The Prayers of the People / Great Litany (Rom 8:26; Jas 5:14): intercessions manifest the Church’s carrying vocation—names spoken aloud as a form of ecclesial presence (Rom 8:26–27).
    • Anointing of the Sick (Jas 5:14–15): the Church’s prayer is bodily and sacramental; suffering is met with oil, hands, and intercession rather than with abstraction.
    • Funeral rites (Rom 8:38–39): the liturgy speaks inseparability in the face of death, confessing that love’s covenantal permanence exceeds the grave.

    Practical Outcomes: [pastoral/catechetical/ethical]

    • Pastoral: normalize psalmic lament in congregational prayer (Ps 22:1–2) so the suffering are not forced into false speech; train caregivers to speak “comfort” as presence rather than as explanation (2 Cor 1:3–4).
    • Catechetical: teach the Church that prayer is Trinitarian mediation: to pray is to be carried by the Spirit into the Son’s intercession before the Father (Rom 8:26–27; Heb 7:25).
    • Ethical: cultivate concrete bearing: visitation, meals, financial aid, advocacy—so that intercession is not disembodied but enacted covenantal love (2 Cor 1:4; Jas 2:15–17).
    • Formation: integrate the daily ordo in affliction:
    • Offer: present the suffering body as living sacrifice without romanticizing pain (Rom 12:1; 1 Pet 4:19).
    • Open Nous: pray for illumined perception that the covenant holds even in darkness (Luke 24:45; Rom 8:28).
    • Discern/Love: discern the next faithful act—often small—under the weight of weakness (Rom 12:2; Jas 1:5).
    • Do: enact the discerned good in dependence on God’s power, not on emotional readiness (2 Cor 1:9–10; Phil 2:13).

    Objections & Replies (3–5 strongest)

    1) Objection: “Lament sounds like unbelief; faithful prayer should be confident, not accusatory.”
    Reply: Psalm 22 is canonical prayer, and it begins with complaint addressed to “my God” (Ps 22:1). Scripture reveals that lament can be covenantal fidelity refusing to cease speaking to God (Ps 22:2–5). Christ himself prays this psalm in the Passion (Mark 15:34), authorizing lament as participation in his own faithful suffering.

    2) Objection: “Romans 8 says the Spirit intercedes; therefore human intercession is unnecessary.”
    Reply: The Spirit’s intercession grounds and animates the Church’s intercession rather than replacing it (Rom 8:26–27). Paul repeatedly commands and models intercession for the saints (Rom 15:30; Eph 6:18–19), which means the Spirit constitutes the Church as a praying Body precisely by carrying our weakness.

    3) Objection: “Suffering is purely individual; the Church cannot ‘carry’ what it does not experience.”
    Reply: Paul explicitly describes suffering and consolation as shared realities: “if we are afflicted, it is for your comfort… if we are comforted, it is for your comfort” (2 Cor 1:6). The Church participates in one Body (1 Cor 12:26), and therefore shared prayer and presence are not intrusions but ecclesial obligations shaped by covenantal love (Jas 5:16).

    4) Objection: “To interpret suffering as ‘participation’ risks sanctifying injustice or discouraging healing.”
    Reply: Romans 8 distinguishes between present groaning and future liberation (Rom 8:21–23); it never calls evil good. Participation means suffering is brought into Christ’s intercession and hope, not that injustice is accepted. Scripture commands prayer for healing and embodied care (Jas 5:14–15; Matt 25:36), which is precisely the Church’s carrying action.

    5) Objection: “If nothing can separate us from God’s love (Rom 8:39), then the felt absence in Ps 22 is theologically incoherent.”
    Reply: Scripture differentiates covenantal reality from perceived experience: the psalm’s cry names the experiential darkness (Ps 22:1–2) while the psalm’s memory confesses God’s fidelity (Ps 22:3–5). Romans 8:39 supplies the doctrinal horizon: the felt absence is real as experience, but it is not the final truth of covenantal communion in Christ.
