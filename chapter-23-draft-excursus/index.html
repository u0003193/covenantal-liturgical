    Chapter 23 Excursus — Eschatological and Kingdom Age: The Logic of the "Already, But Not Yet"

    Purpose: This excursus follows Chapter 23 (From Sabbath to Lord’s Day: Resurrection, Ascension, New Creation, and Heavenly Liturgy) and deepens the theological logic of the “Already, But Not Yet”—the Church’s weekly participation in the inaugurated Kingdom, grounded in Christ’s resurrection and ascension, and shaped by the Lord’s Day as the bridge between present and future. It clarifies how biblical time, liturgical practice, and patristic witness converge to form the Church’s eschatological identity and endurance.

    Thesis

    The “Already, But Not Yet” is the dynamic tension of the Eighth Day: the “Power of the Age to Come” has been inaugurated by the Resurrection but not yet consummated in the final renewal. The Lord’s Day is the weekly bridge where the Church participates in the future while still in the present, forming a people who live from the horizon of the Kingdom.

    I. The Foundation: Sabbath, Time, and the Mosaic Economy

    1) Sabbath as Cosmological and Pedagogical Time

    In the Mosaic economy, time was primarily cosmological and pedagogical. The Sabbath was a weekly “cease-fire” in the world of production, pointing back to the Creator (Gen 2:1–3; Exod 20:8–11). It was the climax of the old creation—a sign of rest continually sought but never permanently possessed, as evidenced by the cycle of sacrifice and the failure of “rest” in the land (Heb 4:8).

    2) The Temporal Gap and the Shadow of Sabbath

    The Israelite looked back to creation and forward to a distant promise, but inhabited a present still dominated by the “toil” of the fall. The Sabbath was a shadow—a repetitive cycle of rest that looked back to a lost Eden and forward to a hoped-for future, but never fully bridged the temporal gap (Lev 23:3; Deut 5:12–15).

    II. The Fulfillment: Christological Bridge and the Eighth Day

    1) Christ as Lord of the Sabbath and Firstborn of New Creation

    Jesus bridges the temporal gap by becoming the “Lord of the Sabbath” (Matt 12:8) and the Firstborn of the New Creation (Col 1:18). His resurrection on the “First Day” launches a new age: He is the “Already” of the Kingdom—the first piece of the future that has entered the present (John 20:1, 19; Rev 1:10).

    2) Ascension and Eschatological Symmetry

    By His Ascension, Christ anchors humanity “inside the veil” (Heb 6:19–20), creating a new eschatological symmetry: we are physically in “This Age,” but our life is “hidden with Christ in God” (Col 3:3) in the “Age to Come.” The Lord’s Day becomes the sacramental time-form of this overlap.

    3) The Eighth Day: Weekly Participation in the Age to Come

    Early Christians called Sunday the “Eighth Day”—the day beyond the seven-day cycle of this world. It is the day where the Heavenly Liturgy (Rev 4–5) and the Earthly Gathering intersect. Every Lord’s Day gathering is a “proleptic ascent”: the Church approaches Zion (Heb 12:22), practicing living in the “Already” while awaiting the “Not Yet.”

    III. The Participation: Sacramental and Ecclesial Reality

    1) Lord’s Day as Sacrament of Time

    The Lord’s Day is not merely a break from the world; it is the sacrament of time. The Church gathers to participate in the future, approaching Zion in worship (Heb 12:22–24), and training the nous to recognize that the “Not Yet” is closer than it appears.

    2) Weekly Dawn: Resurrection Light Breaking into the Present Age

    Time is no longer a circle but an overlap—a weekly “dawn” where the resurrection light of the New Jerusalem breaks into the darkness of the present age. The Lord’s Day transforms from a “duty of the law” into an “embassy of the future,” enabling the Church to endure suffering in “This Age” because she is fed by the reality of the “Age to Come.”

    3) Practical/Noetic Outcome: Formation for Endurance and Hope

    The Church’s weekly participation in the Lord’s Day forms endurance, hope, and glad obedience. It is the time-shape of resurrection and ascension participation, not an arbitrary schedule but the weekly pedagogy of new creation life (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor 16:2).

    IV. Scriptural Event and Claim: Resurrection, Ascension, and Weekly Assembly

    1) Resurrection Inaugurates New Creation

    John’s Gospel highlights the “first day” as a dawn: the crucified Jesus is raised, and a new world begins (John 20:1). The resurrection is not only the vindication of Jesus; it is the beginning of a renewed creation order. Early Christian speech reaches for “eighth day” imagery—not because seven-day creation is rejected, but because creation is being renewed from within.

    2) Ascension Universalizes Access

    The ascended Christ is not absent; He is enthroned. Hebrews’ sanctuary language and Ephesians’ “seated with him” (Eph 2:6) signal that Christ’s humanity has entered the sphere of God’s presence as our representative head. This is the condition for the Church’s universal participation: the way is opened, not locally in Jerusalem only, but in the risen and reigning Lord.

    3) Weekly Assembly as Enacted Approach to Zion

    Hebrews gives the decisive interpretive frame: in worship the Church does not merely imagine heaven; it is brought near to a heavenly assembly—“you have come to Mount Zion” (Heb 12:22). Gathering is the enacted form of “draw near.” Sacred time is shaped by approach—made possible by resurrection and ascension.

    V. Patristic Witness: The Fathers on Eschatological Time and the Lord’s Day

    1) Justin Martyr: Creation and Resurrection in Sunday Worship

    Justin Martyr describes Christian worship on “the day called Sunday” because it gathers both creation and resurrection into one confession: God began the world’s light on the first day, and Christ’s resurrection is the new creation’s light. Sunday is the weekly “place in time” where the Church receives the risen Lord’s gifts.

    2) Ignatius of Antioch: Living According to the Lord’s Day

    Ignatius speaks of Christians as those who have come to live “according to the Lord’s Day”—not only as an appointment but as a mode of existence. Lord’s Day life is a way of inhabiting time shaped by resurrection—time oriented toward communion, obedience, and hope.

    3) Irenaeus, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Maximus: Eighth Day and New Creation

    Later patristic “eighth day” language (Irenaeus, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Maximus) strengthens the same point: the Lord’s Day signifies the eschatological renewal of creation already begun in Christ, the beginning of the age to come within the present age. The Fathers treat Sunday not as policy but as confession: resurrection has re-created time, and the Church lives according to that new time.

    VI. Doctrinal Synthesis: Sabbath Transfigured, Lord’s Day Fulfilled

    1) Sabbath as Cosmological/Covenantal Pedagogy

    Sabbath is creation rest and covenant pedagogy—God gives rest as gift; time is ordered toward communion rather than endless production.

    2) Lord’s Day as Eschatological Sign

    The Lord’s Day is resurrection dawn and new creation sign—time now bears the stamp of the age to come breaking into the present. Because Christ is ascended and enthroned, Lord’s Day worship is not an attempt to climb into heaven; it is the Church’s participation in a heavenly reality opened to it—“draw near,” “you have come.” Sacred time is neither legal burden nor private mood; it is the Church’s received rhythm of new creation life.

    3) Lord’s Day as Weekly Covenant Renewal

    Lord’s Day is Sabbath pedagogy fulfilled and intensified: the weekly time-form of resurrection and ascension participation. It is the time-shape of new creation communion.

    VII. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications: Formation by Eschatological Time

    1) Rest as Communion

    Lord’s Day rest is not first about doing nothing; it is about being gathered into the life of Another. The body learns that it is not sustained by frantic output but by received gift—Word heard, Table received, prayers offered.

    2) Nous Retrained to Live from Zion’s Horizon

    Weekly worship re-centers perception. Instead of reading life from anxiety and scarcity, the believer learns to interpret the week from the enthroned Lamb—“you have come.” This is a discipline against despair and delusion.

    3) Time as Medicine for Disordered Desire

    Because desire is trained by repetition, Lord’s Day reorders desire toward God, neighbor, and the common good. It interrupts the liturgies of consumption and self-salvation by inserting a different weekly story: resurrection, access, communion, sending.

    VIII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location: Word · Table · Prayer in Eschatological Time

    The Lord’s Day is the time-form of Word · Table · Prayer—the Church’s central ordo:

    • Word: The risen Christ addresses his people; Scripture is proclaimed not from a distance but within opened access.
    • Table: Communion is received as the Church’s embodied participation in the once-for-all Paschal mystery.
    • Prayer: Intercession rises as participation in Christ’s priestly session, training the Church’s desires outward toward neighbor and world.

    This weekly assembly is not mere inspiration. It is covenant renewal in eschatological time: the people are gathered as one body, reconciled, fed, and then dismissed as those who carry “heaven on earth” not as private ecstasy but as embodied mercy, vocation, and witness throughout the week.

    IX. Practical Outcomes and Pastoral Implications

    • Catechesis: Teach the Lord’s Day as resurrection/ascension participation (“you have come”), not as arbitrary policy or mere continuity dispute.
    • Spiritual formation: Train weekly practices (gathering, resting, receiving Word/Table/Prayer) as medicine against anxiety, activism, and isolation.
    • Pastoral care: For the exhausted, preach rest as communion rather than mere leisure; for the lax, teach gathering as obedience to the opened-access reality (Heb 10:25).
    • Ecclesial unity: Emphasize Sunday assembly as one-body formation (not individualized spirituality).
    • Mission: Frame dismissal as sending: the Church carries “heaven on earth” through vocation, mercy, and witness across the week.

    X. Objections & Replies

    • Objection: Isn’t this Judaizing—turning Sunday into a new law?
    • Reply: The claim is not that Christians re-enter Mosaic legislation unchanged, but that resurrection and ascension transfigure sacred time into eschatological participation. The Lord’s Day is gospel-shaped: it is received as gift and enacted as communion, not imposed as merit.
    • Objection: Doesn’t this bind consciences by making weekly gathering necessary?
    • Reply: Hebrews itself grounds assembly in opened access and mutual encouragement (Heb 10:19–25). The aim is not legalism but the protection of the Church’s participatory life: we gather because the risen Lord gives himself to his body, and because we need one another to endure.
    • Objection: If worship is heavenly participation, isn’t it spiritual escapism?
    • Reply: Hebrews connects heavenly approach with perseverance and embodied holiness (Heb 12). “You have come” strengthens endurance and fuels mercy; it does not bypass the world.

    • Before: We are waiting for the Kingdom to start; Sunday is a break from the world.
    • After: The Kingdom has already started in the Risen Christ; Sunday is the weekly “immersion” into that already-present reality.
