    Prologue Frame: The Throne–Ladder Mystery — The Name of Jesus as Heaven–Earth Contact

    Thesis

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts & Secondary corroboration)

    Gen 28:10–17; Ex 25:17–22; Deut 12:5–11; John 1:14, 51; Heb 10:19–22; Heb 12:22–24; Acts 2:21; Rom 10:9–13; Phil 2:9–11; Rev 4–5; Rev 8:3–4.

    Num 7:89; 1 Kgs 8:27–30; John 2:19–21; Heb 8:5; Rev 11:19.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    The Church does not approach God as a disembodied mind seeking ideas, nor as a technician manipulating sacred tools. Scripture instead narrates a covenantal drama of contact: God places his presence, God speaks from mercy, God opens access in Christ, and God gathers a people into heavenly communion.

    The modern imagination regularly collapses “spiritual” into the mental: ideas about God, interior experiences, private intention. When that collapse occurs, Scripture’s ascent/temple/Name grammar is heard as metaphor or ornament, rather than as the Church’s public language for heaven–earth meeting. The result is confusion about prayer, worship, and formation: either “presence” becomes a mood or “ritual” becomes a mechanism.

    Question at issue: What does it mean, biblically and ecclesially, to “enter the heavenly” and to “call on the Name,” and where is the concrete contact point where heaven touches earth now—without reductionism and without technique?

    The prologue begins by naming the real pressure: “spiritual” is reduced to the mental, so heaven–earth language becomes either metaphor or mechanism. The prologue asks where Scripture locates real contact and real entry.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: “spiritual” means private interior uplift or controllable technique.
    After: “spiritual” means covenantal participation in Christ’s real access, centered in his Name.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Two opposite errors arise when “spiritual” is reduced to the mental.

    1. Reductionism: heavenly language becomes interior uplift; temple and ascent become symbols only. But Hebrews does not speak as if Zion were a metaphor for mood; it declares, “you have come” (Heb 12:22). And it grounds entry not in an experience we generate, but in priestly access opened “through the curtain…his flesh” (Heb 10:20).

    2. Instrumentalism: because the supernatural has been thinned into subjectivity, words and rites are reimagined as mechanisms—actions that “work” automatically. But Paul binds calling on the Name to faith and confession—“Jesus is Lord”—and Hebrews binds entering to Christ’s mediation, not to ritual performance (Rom 10:9–13; Heb 10:19–22). The Name is efficacious because the Lord is living and enthroned, not because syllables have power.

    Both errors misunderstand the Church’s participation. The correction must be canonical and christological: the Name of Jesus is neither a metaphor nor a mechanism, but the concrete covenantal site of access and communion.

    Reductionism evacuates the Name into interiority; instrumentalism turns the Name into technique. Scripture rejects both by grounding “entry” in Christ’s flesh and “calling” in faith’s confession of the enthroned Jesus.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: the Name is either “just language” or “verbal power.”
    After: the Name of Jesus names living mediation—access and communion given in covenant.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    Scripture answers the question not by an abstract definition but by a canonical sequence—a chain of contact that converges on a person.

    1) Gate: Bethel and heaven–earth traffic as gift

    Jacob beholds the stairway and confesses, “this is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven” (Gen 28:17). The scene is covenantal: the Lord binds promise to Jacob’s exile (Gen 28:13–15). Heaven–earth contact is God’s gift, not Jacob’s technique.

    The Gospel reads Bethel christologically. Jesus promises Nathanael: “you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man” (John 1:51). The ladder is not a method of ascent; it is a mediator. Heaven is opened in the person who will bear the supreme Name.

    2) Throne: Mercy Seat as meeting and speech under mercy

    The Lord attaches presence and speech to the Mercy Seat: “There I will meet with you…and I will speak with you” (Ex 25:22; cf. Num 7:89). Encounter is ordered as covenantal meeting under mercy. Presence is given, located, and governed by God’s promise.

    3) Name: covenantal presence where God dwells and hears

    Deuteronomy describes the chosen place as the place where God “puts his Name” and gathers his people to rejoice before him (Deut 12:5–7, 11–12). Solomon confesses that God cannot be contained, yet pleads that God will hear prayer oriented toward the house (1 Kgs 8:27–30). The temple is not a container; it is a covenantal site of divine attention. The Name signifies presence and rule.

    4) Veil/Flesh: contact opened in Christ’s priestly mediation

    John announces that the Word “tabernacled” among us (John 1:14) and that Jesus’ body is the true temple (John 2:19–21). Hebrews provides the decisive synthesis: we have “confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus…through the curtain, that is, through his flesh” (Heb 10:19–20). Here heaven–earth contact is anchored in the incarnate mediator: spiritual and material meet in Christ’s flesh, and access is priestly rather than psychological.

    5) Zion and throne‑room liturgy: the Church’s worship as participation in heavenly reality

    Hebrews speaks in the perfect tense of ecclesial arrival: “you have come to Mount Zion…to innumerable angels… and to Jesus” (Heb 12:22–24). Revelation displays what Hebrews names: the heavenly throne‑room liturgy (Rev 4–5) and the prayers of the saints rising as incense before God (Rev 8:3–4). The Church’s worship is not a metaphor for heaven; it is participation in the heavenly assembly gathered around the Lamb.

    6) Calling on the Name: confession‑participation in the exalted Lord

    Pentecost proclamation makes the logic explicit: “Everyone who calls upon the Name of the Lord shall be saved” (Acts 2:21). Paul clarifies that this calling is not magical speech but covenantal confession: to call on the Name is to confess “Jesus is Lord” and believe in his resurrection (Rom 10:9–13). Philippians locates the Name within Christ’s exaltation: God “bestowed on him the Name that is above every name,” so that every tongue confesses his lordship (Phil 2:9–11).

    The canonical chain therefore converges on a single claim: the Name of Jesus is the concrete contact point—the personal mediator in whom heaven is opened, mercy speaks, access is granted, the Church draws near, and worship becomes communion.

    Scripture’s chain of contact (gate–throne–Name–veil/flesh–Zion–throne-room worship) culminates in Jesus: the ladder becomes a person, access becomes priestly, and calling on his Name becomes faith’s entry into heavenly communion.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: ascent and temple language are either metaphor or mechanism.
    After: ascent and temple language name real contact in Christ, enacted as calling on his Name in faith.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The Fathers receive this canonical chain as a unified mystery: worship joins the heavenly assembly, yet always as covenantal gift in Christ, never as technique.

    • Cyril of Jerusalem teaches the faithful to approach the mysteries as ecclesial participation rather than private speculation: the Church learns the reality of Christ’s saving presence as she confesses and receives, not as she invents an ascent.
    • Basil of Caesarea clarifies the Spirit’s divine agency so that invocation does not become mechanism: God acts personally; the Church prays and receives.
    • John Chrysostom insists that the truth of altar participation is tested by mercy: the worship that invokes Christ cannot despise Christ in the poor—contact with the heavenly must yield earthly charity.
    • Irenaeus reads Israel’s cult as real pedagogy fulfilled in Christ, guarding against both empty symbolism and parallel sacrifice: the same God forms one people through one economy.
    • Origen trains the Church’s nous to perceive Christ as Scripture’s scope: Bethel, temple, and ascent converge on the Logos-made-flesh, not on a method of self-elevation.
    • Augustine grounds sacrifice in charity: the true sacrifice is the Church offered in Christ; invocation of Christ’s Name is ordered toward love, not toward spiritual display.

    Patristic witness holds together liturgical realism and pastoral guardrails: the Name of Jesus is invoked as covenantal gift in the Spirit, verified by mercy, and protected from superstition and empty symbolism.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: worship either lacks reality (symbolism) or becomes controllable (superstition).
    After: worship is real participation in Christ, guarded by Spirit-agency and tested by charity.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The prologue’s doctrinal resolution can be stated as a Two‑Step Name Thesis governed by Christ’s priestly mediation.

    Step 1 — The Name as covenantal presence (Temple‑logic).
    In the Old Testament, God “puts his Name” where he chooses to dwell and to hear the prayers of his people (Deut 12:5–11; 1 Kgs 8:27–30). The Name signifies covenantal presence and rule: God binds himself by promise to meet, speak, forgive, and gather.

    Step 2 — The Name of Jesus as the personal contact point (Christ‑logic).
    In the New Testament, Jesus bears the supreme Name (Phil 2:9–11). Calling on the Name is faith’s confession and reliance—“Jesus is Lord”—by which the Church participates in Christ’s priestly access opened “through the veil…his flesh” (Rom 10:9–13; Heb 10:19–22). The Name is efficacious because it names the living Lord who mediates communion; it is not efficacious as verbal technique.

    Therefore the Name of Jesus is the contact point of:

    • Heaven and earth: heaven is opened on the Son of Man (John 1:51), and the Church has “come” to Zion and to Jesus (Heb 12:22–24).
    • Time and eternity: Christ’s once‑for‑all offering opens abiding access (Heb 10:10–14, 19–22), so the Church’s worship shares in the heavenly reality that does not pass away.
    • Spiritual and material: access is “through the veil…his flesh” (Heb 10:20); communion is not disembodied uplift but incarnational participation.

    “Entry into the heavenly,” then, is not private mysticism and not ecclesial technology. It is covenantal participation in Christ through his Name: promised, mediated, received in faith, enacted in the Church.

    Doctrinally, God’s Name signifies covenantal presence, and Jesus bears the supreme Name as personal mediator. Calling on his Name is the Church’s faith-filled participation in priestly access—real contact across heaven/earth, time/eternity, and spiritual/material.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: the Name is a label or a lever.
    After: the Name of Jesus is living mediation—covenantal presence and priestly access received in faith.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    If the Name of Jesus is the contact point of heaven and earth, then spirituality cannot be reduced to interior mood. It involves the renewal of perception and the re‑ordering of the body’s obedience.

    Christ must “open the mind (nous) to understand the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45). That opening is not mere intellectual upgrade; it is a re‑location of what counts as real. The Christian learns to perceive the world within the covenantal drama: promise, mercy, access, communion. Calling on the Name of Jesus is therefore not merely saying a word; it is a repeated return of the heart to the mediator—confession, reliance, and doxology.

    This is why Paul describes the Christian life as “reasonable worship”: “present your bodies as a living sacrifice…be transformed by the renewal of your mind” (Rom 12:1–2). The Church’s corporate entry into the heavenly becomes personal liturgy: obedience from the heart, mercy in the body, and attention gathered toward Christ rather than dispersed into anxiety and control.

    The Name of Jesus trains the nous to see Scripture and the world truthfully and extends worship into bodily obedience. Spirituality is participation that remakes perception and makes the body a living sacrifice.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: “spiritual” is mainly interior experience.
    After: “spiritual” is renewed perception and embodied obedience grounded in Christ’s Name and access.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church’s Word–Table–Prayer is the enacted entry into the heavenly precisely because it is the Church’s repeated calling on the Name of Jesus in covenantal form.

    • Word: Christ addresses his people; the Spirit opens the nous so the Church hears Scripture as living covenantal speech and learns to confess the Lord rightly (Luke 24:45).
    • Table: the Church draws near through the opened way “through the veil…his flesh” (Heb 10:20). The Supper is not technique but communion: the Church receives Christ’s gift as those who belong to his Name and proclaim his death until he comes (1 Cor 11:26).
    • Prayer: the Church calls on the Name as incense in the heavenly sanctuary, joining the saints’ intercession and the Church’s doxology to the throne‑room worship of the Lamb (Rev 8:3–4; Rev 5:8–14).

    Week by week, the Church’s worship re-installs reality: heaven is opened in Christ, access is given, and the Name of Jesus gathers a people into communion. The liturgy trains the Church to live from gift before task, so that participation issues in covenantal love (Heb 13:15–16).

    The ordo is the Church’s stable calling on the Name of Jesus: Word opens perception, Table grants communion through Christ’s flesh, and Prayer joins heavenly worship—forming a people whose entry is verified in love.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: liturgy is either empty symbol or spiritual technology.
    After: liturgy is covenantal entry—calling on Jesus’ Name as real communion with the heavenly assembly.

    Patristic Witness (Top 6, SBL)

    1. Cyril of Jerusalem. Catecheses mystagogicae (esp. 5.6–10).
    2. Basil of Caesarea. De Spiritu Sancto (esp. 9.22; 16.38).
    3. John Chrysostom. Homiliae (various; altar-logic and mercy).
    4. Irenaeus. Adversus haereses IV (pedagogy and fulfillment).
    5. Origen. Homiliae (Genesis/Exodus; christological scope).
    6. Augustine. De civitate Dei X (true sacrifice; worship ordered to charity).

    Practical Outcomes

    • Catechesis: teach “spiritual” as covenantal participation: the Name of Jesus is the Church’s contact point with heaven, not a metaphor for inner life (Heb 12:22–24; Phil 2:9–11).
    • Prayer: teach calling on Jesus’ Name as confession + reliance, resisting both self-generated uplift and verbal technique (Rom 10:9–13).
    • Formation: locate nous‑training inside Word–Table–Prayer so perception and desire are re-aimed toward Christ (Luke 24:45; Rom 12:1–2).
    • Ethics: bind altar and neighbor: entry into the heavenly is verified in mercy and sharing as “sacrifices pleasing to God” (Heb 13:15–16).

    Objections & Replies

    1. Objection: “Heavenly entry is metaphorical only.”
      Reply: Hebrews grounds entry in priestly mediation “through the curtain…his flesh” (Heb 10:19–20) and declares the assembled Church has “come to Mount Zion… and to Jesus” (Heb 12:22–24). The claim is ecclesial and christological, not merely psychological.

    2. Objection: “Calling on the Name makes prayer a technique.”
      Reply: Calling is bound to faith and confession—“Jesus is Lord”—not to verbal mechanics (Rom 10:9–13). The Name is efficacious because it names the living, enthroned Lord who mediates access (Phil 2:9–11), not because syllables operate automatically.

    3. Objection: “Temple and throne language undermines Christ’s once‑for‑all work.”
      Reply: Hebrews insists Christ’s offering is once for all (Heb 10:10–14). Temple-logic is the grammar of fulfillment: participation is communion in the one sacrifice’s access, never repetition (Heb 10:19–22).

    4. Objection: “This collapses inner spirituality into external ritual.”
      Reply: Scripture’s anthropology is integrated: the nous is opened (Luke 24:45), bodies are offered (Rom 12:1), and discernment is renewed (Rom 12:2) within the Church’s worship and obedience. The Name of Jesus gathers the whole person, and love verifies the reality (Heb 13:16).
