    Introduction: The Christian Life as Covenantal–Liturgical Participation

    Thesis

    The Christian life is covenantal fidelity within Spirit-given participation in God’s presence and speech through liturgy—one Sinai-shaped fabric (Exod 19–Lev 9) fulfilled in Christ who embodies covenant–presence–liturgy, enacted in the Church’s ordo of Word, Table, and Prayer, and personalized as the offering of the body with a renewed nous (Rom 12:1–2; Luke 24:45). Participation is therefore neither private interiority nor ritual technique: it is ecclesial entry into what Christ has opened once for all (Heb 10:19–22), lived “between the ages” as both communion with God now and a foretaste of the age to come then (Heb 12:22–24; 6:4–5; Rev 21–22).

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts )

    The biblical ground functions as architectural framing: Showing the triad (covenant / presence-as-speech / liturgy) as one reality.

    Israel’s integral pattern (Exod 19–Lev 9): covenant • presence/speaking • liturgy
    - Covenant (people formed by divine address): Exod 19:3–6; Exod 24:3–11
    - Presence and speaking “under mercy”: Exod 25:17–22; Num 7:89
    - Liturgy as pedagogy for nearness: Lev 8–9; Ps 99:1–5

    Christ’s fulfillment (covenant • presence • liturgy in person)
    - Luke 24:13–35, 44–49; John 1:14; John 2:19–21; Rom 3:21–26; Heb 9:11–14; Heb 10:19–22

    The Church’s participation (now / history / future held together by Spirit)
    - Acts 2:1–4, 42; 1 Cor 10:16–17; 1 Cor 11:23–26; 1 Cor 3:16–17; Eph 2:19–22; Heb 12:22–24; Rev 4–5; Rev 8:3–4

    The person’s “daily liturgy” (offering • opened nous • obedience-from-heart)
    - Luke 24:45; Rom 12:1–2; Gal 5:16–25; Phil 2:12–13

    Chapter Body

    I. Context & Question at Issue

    In modern Christian speech, “spiritual life” is frequently torn into pieces. Some treat it as covenant without presence: obedience becomes moral performance with little sense that God is speaking and drawing near. Others treat it as presence without covenant: spiritual life becomes interior experience detached from public allegiance and concrete obedience. Still others treat it as liturgy without either: worship becomes an instrument for producing results, as though God’s nearness were a mechanism.

    Scripture presses against all three reductions by giving the Church a concrete, historical whole: Exodus 19 through Leviticus 9. Here covenant, presence, and liturgy are not optional layers but one fabric. God speaks to form a people, binds them in covenant, dwells among them, and trains them by a liturgical grammar that makes nearness possible without presumption. This “Covenant–Presence/Speaking–Liturgy” whole is not left behind in the gospel; it is fulfilled and intensified in Christ, and it becomes the Church’s living form by the Holy Spirit.

    The central modern problem is not merely unbelief but fragmentation: separating covenant, presence, and liturgy into competing “spiritualities.” Scripture answers with an integral pattern—Exod 19–Lev 9—that becomes the key for reading Christ, Church, and the Christian person as one participatory life.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: spiritual life is ethics alone, experience alone, or ritual technique.
    After: spiritual life is one Sinai-shaped fabric—covenant fidelity within Spirit-given participation in God’s presence and speech through liturgy.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Three rival accounts of the Christian life recur precisely where the biblical fabric is forgotten.

    1. Moralism (covenant detached from presence and liturgy). Obedience becomes self-authorization. Yet Sinai’s commands are given within a scene of divine nearness and divine speech, and Hebrews frames Christian existence as “drawing near” because access has been opened (Exod 19; Heb 10:22). Covenant obedience is never a free-floating ethic.

    2. Interiorized spirituality (presence detached from covenant and Church). “Presence” becomes private uplift. But Scripture locates divine indwelling and discernment within a people assembled and addressed (Acts 2:42; 1 Cor 3:16–17). If the Church is God’s dwelling, then spirituality cannot be reduced to the isolated self.

    3. Ritualism (liturgy detached from covenantal promise and personal transformation). Worship is treated as technique. Yet epiclesis is dependence, not control; anamnesis is participation in Christ’s once-for-all gift, not a re-sacrifice (Heb 10:10–14; 1 Cor 10:16–17). Liturgy without renewed obedience and mercy becomes self-contradiction (Heb 10:24–25; 13:16).

    Each reduction fails by dividing what Scripture joins: covenant without presence becomes moralism; presence without covenant becomes interiorism; liturgy without either becomes mechanism. The cure is not “balance” but retrieval of the single Sinai-shaped fabric fulfilled in Christ.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: choose one—ethics, experience, or ritual—as the “real” Christian life.
    After: covenant, presence/speaking, and liturgy are inseparable modes of one participation.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    The Bible’s account of spiritual life is not first a theory but an event: God forms a people by covenant speech and draws them near through mediated presence. Exodus 19–Leviticus 9 is the canonical place where this pattern becomes unmistakable.

    At Sinai, God’s first act is not to ask for effort but to speak and thereby constitute a people: “you shall be my treasured possession… a kingdom of priests” (Exod 19:5–6). Covenant begins as divine address received in public assent—“All that the LORD has spoken we will do” (Exod 24:3). Here covenant is not contract but allegiance shaped by hearing.

    Yet covenant speech moves immediately toward covenant presence. The Lord attaches dwelling and continued speaking to the Mercy Seat: “There I will meet with you… and I will speak with you” (Exod 25:22; cf. Num 7:89). Presence is not a vague atmosphere; it is promised nearness under mercy—God’s throne of meeting where speech continues.

    And the same fabric includes covenant liturgy as pedagogy. Leviticus 8–9 is not a “religious add-on” to covenant; it is the Spiritless form (in the old covenant) of a necessary truth: a people cannot live near the Holy One without a trained pattern of approach—washing, offering, blessing, and communal nearness. Liturgy is thus the shape of covenant life under presence.

    The New Testament claims that this whole is fulfilled—without being discarded—in Christ. The Word “tabernacled” among us (John 1:14), and his body is the Temple (John 2:19–21). Hebrews states the climactic claim: the way into God’s presence is opened “through the veil, that is, through his flesh” (Heb 10:19–20). The once-for-all saving event is not merely past information; it is the definitive opening of access that the Church now lives.

    Therefore the Church’s “now” is not merely psychological: in worship we “have come to Mount Zion… and to Jesus” (Heb 12:22–24). The “history” of Christ’s saving act is proclaimed and participated: “Do this in remembrance (anamnesis) of me… you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:24–26). And the “future” presses into the present as foretaste: believers taste “the powers of the age to come” (Heb 6:4–5), and the Eucharist is both communion and anticipation.

    Exod 19–Lev 9 reveals one inseparable fabric—covenant, presence-as-speaking, and liturgy-as-nearness—fulfilled and intensified in Christ’s flesh, and lived by the Church as real participation now, proclamation of the saving past, and foretaste of the age to come.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: covenant is law, presence is mood, liturgy is optional.
    After: covenant–presence–liturgy is one biblical life, fulfilled in Christ and enacted in the Church’s participation across past, now, and future.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The Fathers do not “add” to Scripture; they show the Church learning to speak faithfully where reduction always threatens.

    • Irenaeus reads Israel’s covenantal history as divine pedagogy: God forms humanity by stages toward communion and likeness. The Sinai pattern is therefore not obsolete scaffolding but a true preparation whose reality is fulfilled in Christ and extended in the Church’s life (Against Heresies IV).

    • Athanasius insists on the ontological weight of fulfillment: the Word becomes flesh so that human beings may be remade for divine communion. If the goal is real participation, then presence cannot be reduced to symbol, and sanctification cannot be reduced to moral effort (On the Incarnation 54).

    • Basil guards the Spirit’s agency: God’s presence and action in worship are personal and divine, not a manipulable force. This protects both covenant (gift before task) and liturgy (dependence, not technique) (On the Holy Spirit).

    • Cyril of Jerusalem offers mystagogy: the Church learns Scripture’s meaning by entering it. The mysteries are not private experiences; they are ecclesial participation in Christ’s saving work, received with reverence and enacted in the Body (Mystagogical Catecheses).

    • Chrysostom supplies the necessary test: the altar cannot be severed from mercy. Participation in Christ’s Body and Blood demands reconciliation and generosity; otherwise worship becomes self-contradiction (Homilies).

    Taken together, the Fathers protect the same integral whole you see in Exod 19–Lev 9: covenant fidelity, divine presence and speech, and liturgy as the Church’s training in nearness—now fulfilled in Christ and empowered by the Spirit.

    The Fathers function as the Church’s interpretive guardrails: they resist moralism, interiorism, and mechanism by insisting that Sinai’s covenant–presence–liturgy is fulfilled in Christ and enacted as ecclesial participation that bears ethical fruit.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: Fathers are decorative citations for ideas we already hold.
    After: Fathers are witnesses to Scripture’s integral logic—how the Church learned to live covenant–presence–liturgy in Christ.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    We can now state the book’s doctrinal resolution as a single claim with four inseparable layers.

    1. Israel (Exod 19–Lev 9): covenant–presence/speaking–liturgy as one fabric.
      God forms a people by speech, binds them in covenant allegiance, dwells among them, and trains them by a liturgy that makes nearness possible. Covenant is not intelligible without presence; presence is not sustained without liturgical approach; liturgy is not meaningful without covenant.

    2. Christ: covenant–presence–liturgy embodied.
      Christ does not merely teach covenant; he is the covenant’s mediator and fulfillment. He is not merely a bearer of presence; he is the Word made flesh, the true Temple. He is not merely a liturgical example; he is the High Priest and the once-for-all offering by which access is opened (Heb 9–10). In him, the three are not separated: covenant life, divine presence, and true worship meet in one person.

    3. Church: covenant–presence–liturgy participated (Word–Table–Prayer).
      By the Spirit, the Church becomes God’s dwelling (1 Cor 3:16–17; Eph 2:22). In the ordo of Word, Table, and Prayer, the Church participates in Christ’s saving event: proclaiming the past (“until he comes”), entering the “now” of heavenly Zion, and tasting the future powers of the age to come (1 Cor 11:26; Heb 12:22–24; Heb 6:4–5). The Church’s worship is therefore neither repetition nor mere memory, but Spirit-given participation.

    4. Person: covenant–presence–liturgy personalized as “daily offering” with renewed nous.
      The same pattern descends into the believer’s daily life without becoming individualism. Romans 12:1–2 names the personal form of participation: the body offered as living sacrifice (a personal liturgy), the mind (nous) renewed for discernment, and obedience rendered from the heart as covenant fidelity. Luke 24:45 supplies the necessary grace: Christ must open the nous to understand Scripture, so that the day itself becomes intelligible within the covenantal drama.

    This is the book’s governing “rule of reading”: one Sinai-shaped fabric, fulfilled in Christ, enacted in the Church, and lived personally as offered obedience with illumined perception—always tending toward the telos of covenantal love.

    The covenant–presence–liturgy triad is distinct yet inseparable across four layers: Israel’s pedagogy, Christ’s embodiment, the Church’s participation, and the person’s daily offering with renewed nous.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: Israel is “background,” Christ is “doctrine,” Church is “community,” person is “application.”
    After: one fabric runs through all four—covenant fidelity within participation in God’s presence and worship.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    If covenant–presence–liturgy is one fabric, then spiritual formation is not primarily the acquisition of religious information or the management of private states. It is the training of the whole person for participation.

    Two scriptural claims are decisive. First, the risen Christ “opened their minds (nous) to understand the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45). This names the basic need: without an opened nous, the covenantal drama remains opaque, and the Church’s worship becomes either habit or technique. Second, Paul commands: “present your bodies… be transformed by the renewal of your mind (nous)” (Rom 12:1–2). The offering of the body and the renewal of the nous belong together: worship is personal, embodied, and discerning.

    Therefore the Christian person participates in the same triad: - Covenant: daily allegiance—obedience as faith’s response, not self-generated heroism. - Presence/speaking: attentiveness to God’s address in Scripture and in the Church’s proclamation. - Liturgy: not only Sunday ordo, but the personal liturgy of presenting the body, practicing repentance, and walking by the Spirit so that obedience becomes “from the heart.”

    The result is not moralism, because the Spirit forms what he commands (Gal 5:22–25; Phil 2:12–13). And it is not private mysticism, because the nous is opened and trained within the Church’s public worship and sacramental life.

    Formation names the personal enactment of the same Sinai-shaped fabric: offered bodies and renewed nous make covenant fidelity possible as living participation, not as technique or performance.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: discipleship is willpower plus religious input.
    After: discipleship is Spirit-given participation—opened nous and offered body—so obedience becomes covenantal life.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church is the place where covenant–presence–liturgy becomes public, stable, and transmissible—because the Church is not merely a gathering of like-minded individuals but the Spirit-constituted dwelling of God.

    In the ordo of Word, Table, and Prayer, the Church lives in three tenses at once:

    • The past (“history”): participation in Christ’s saving event. In the Lord’s Supper the Church does not escape history; she proclaims and participates in the definitive event: “you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26). The saving act remains once for all (Heb 10:10–14), yet it is not locked away in the past.

    • The now: entry into the heavenly throne room. Hebrews dares to say, in worship, “you have come to Mount Zion… and to Jesus” (Heb 12:22–24). Revelation’s throne-room worship (Rev 4–5) and the incense-prayers (Rev 8:3–4) disclose what the Church is doing: she is gathered into Christ’s priestly access, approaching the Father through the Son in the Spirit.

    • The future: foretaste of the age to come. The Church tastes the powers of the coming age (Heb 6:4–5) and receives communion as anticipation of the unveiled city where the Lamb is the lamp (Rev 21–22).

    This is why the Introduction must insist on the inseparable three. If the Church loses covenant, liturgy becomes aesthetic. If she loses presence and divine speaking, liturgy becomes empty routine. If she loses liturgy, covenant becomes moralism and presence becomes interiorism. But when the fabric is held together, worship becomes participation, and participation becomes love.

    The Church’s ordo is the concrete location where covenant fidelity, divine presence and speech, and liturgical approach cohere across time—past saving event, present throne-room entry, and future foretaste.

    Frame Shift (Before → After):
    Before: worship is remembrance, inspiration, or religious duty.
    After: worship is Spirit-given participation in Christ’s access—holding together covenant, presence, and liturgy as one life.

    Practical Outcomes

    • Pastoral: Diagnose fragmentation (moralism/interiorism/mechanism) as a severing of what Scripture holds together; re-teach Christian life as covenant fidelity within participation in divine presence through worship.
    • Catechetical: Teach Exod 19–Lev 9 as an integral whole—covenant • presence/speaking • liturgy—and show how Christ embodies the three and the Church/person participate the three by the Spirit.
    • Ethical: Make “altar-logic” explicit: covenant participation in Christ’s presence must issue in mercy, reconciliation, and justice (Heb 10:24–25; 13:16).
    • Formation: Give a repeatable rule: offer the body → pray for opened nous → discern/love God’s will → enact it (Rom 12:1–2; Luke 24:45), as the personal extension of the Church’s ordo.

    Objections & Replies

    1. Objection: “If covenant–presence–liturgy are inseparable, doesn’t that make Christian life ‘too liturgical’?”
      Reply: Scripture’s own covenant life is liturgically enacted (Exod 24:3–11), and Hebrews commands the Church to assemble precisely because access is opened (Heb 10:19–25). Liturgy is not decoration; it is the form covenant life takes near the Holy One.

    2. Objection: “Does participation in the Supper imply repeating Christ’s sacrifice?”
      Reply: Hebrews forbids repetition (Heb 10:10–14), yet Paul calls the cup and bread communion in Christ (1 Cor 10:16–17) and says the Church proclaims the Lord’s death until he comes (1 Cor 11:26). Participation is incorporation into the once-for-all event’s living efficacy, not an addition to it.

    3. Objection: “Isn’t ‘presence’ subjective—just how I feel?”
      Reply: Scripture locates presence in God’s covenantal initiative: “There I will meet with you… and I will speak with you” (Exod 25:22). In the New Covenant, presence is mediated through Christ’s priestly access and the Spirit’s indwelling of the Church (Heb 10:19–22; 1 Cor 3:16–17), not through fluctuating affect.

    4. Objection: “Does ‘offering the body’ (Rom 12:1) turn Christianity into moral striving?”
      Reply: Romans 12:1–2 follows Romans 1–11: the offering is response to mercy, not a means to earn it. The renewed nous and the Spirit’s work ensure that obedience is fruit of participation, not autonomous achievement (Gal 5:22–25; Phil 2:13).

    • Beale, G. K. The Temple and the Church’s Mission: A Biblical Theology of the Dwelling Place of God. Downers Grove, IL: IVP Academic, 2004.
      A rigorous biblical-theological account of temple/presence as the Bible’s dwelling-place throughline, supporting Eden→Sinai→Christ→Church→new creation coherence.

    • Daniélou, Jean. The Bible and the Liturgy. Notre Dame, IN: University of Notre Dame Press, 1956.
      Classic ressourcement on typology and liturgy as participation in salvation history, helpful for reading Exod–Lev as pedagogy fulfilled in Christ.

    • de Lubac, Henri. Catholicism: Christ and the Common Destiny of Man. Translated by Lancelot C. Sheppard and Elizabeth Englund. San Francisco: Ignatius Press, 1988.
      Ecclesial and participatory vision of salvation that resists individualism and clarifies the Church as the public site of communion.

    • Schmemann, Alexander. For the Life of the World: Sacraments and Orthodoxy. Crestwood, NY: St Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1973.
      A liturgical theology of participation and eschatological foretaste that harmonizes with the book’s “now / future” dimension.

    • Wilken, Robert Louis. The Spirit of Early Christian Thought: Seeking the Face of God. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2003.
      Exemplifies Scripture-in-the-Church reading and the patristic habit of joining doctrine, worship, and spiritual formation.

    • Bauckham, Richard. The Theology of the Book of Revelation. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1993.
      A clear account of Revelation’s worship-centered eschatology, supporting the CODA’s movement from mediated participation to unveiled communion.
